\title{Improving extension services through non-monetary incentives: Experimental evidence from Ghana}
\author{Kazushi Takahashi \and Wataru Kodama \and Yukichi Mano}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Public agricultural extension services in developing countries often suffer from weak service delivery, yet how to improve agents' performance without monetary incentives remains unclear. We study non-monetary incentives in a randomized controlled trial across eight rice-growing districts in Ghana. Agricultural extension agents received biweekly feedback reports based on farmers' assessments, either alone or combined with sensitization training on the social value of extension work. A cross-randomized GPS-stamp intervention asked agents to record their field visits. Biweekly phone surveys reveal little correlation between agents' self-reported visits and farmers' reports. Farmer feedback improved several dimensions of service delivery. Farmers in both treatment groups reported more visits during the planting season, greater satisfaction with extension services, and a higher likelihood of receiving helpful advice. Over the full survey period, increased visits were observed only in the feedback-plus-training arm. The interventions did not affect agents' intrinsic, extrinsic, or prosocial motivation but were associated with a stronger internal locus of control over agricultural production. We find no detectable effects on rice yields or income, although treated farmers used less seed and herbicide and more hired labor. GPS stamping had no detectable effect. Because treatment was assigned across only eight districts, statistical significance is sensitive to the inference method. Overall, the findings suggest that farmer feedback can improve some dimensions of extension service delivery, while evidence of improvements in farm-level outcomes remains limited.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Addressing global food insecurity and poverty, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa, requires sustained improvements in agricultural productivity. Such improvements depend not only on the development of agricultural technologies but also on their effective dissemination and adoption by farmers. Public agricultural extension services play a central role in this process, as knowledge spillovers among farmers can lead to the under provision of private extension services \citep{Takahashi2020}. Yet public extension services in developing countries often suffer from weak service delivery, including agent absenteeism and infrequent visits to farmers \citep{Anderson2007, Alam2024}. Even when extension agents possess relevant technical knowledge and operational resources, the effectiveness of their services depends on the effort they exert in reaching farmers and supporting technology adoption. This raises a central question: How can extension agents be motivated to deliver more effective services?

This question reflects a broader incentive problem in public service delivery in developing countries. Absenteeism among teachers and health workers is widespread \citep{Chaudhury2006}, and even when present, providers often fail to deliver services commensurate with their knowledge \citep{Das2007, Finan2017}. Because providers’ performance is difficult to measure and monitoring systems are weak in many developing countries, individual effort may be only loosely linked to rewards: working harder often brings neither higher pay nor better promotion prospects, which weakens providers’ incentives to perform well. Previous research has shown that monetary rewards, monitoring, and public recognition can improve service providers' behavior \citep{Muralidharan2011, Duflo2012, Banerjee2008, Ashraf2014, Leaver2021, Mansuri2023}.  
 %and that compensation and prosocial motivation shape who enters public service \citep{Bo2013, Banuri2016}. 
 In addition, providers may exert less effort when they believe that their work is unlikely to improve the lives of those they serve \citep{Kaur2023}, whereas interventions that emphasize the social value of their work can increase effort \citep{Khan2025}. Similar challenges arise in agricultural extension. \citet{Ragasa2013} report that a lack of encouragement from supervisors undermines extension agents' motivation, whereas expressions of gratitude from farmers strengthen it. These findings suggest that non-monetary interventions that communicate farmers' evaluations or emphasize the social value of extension work may encourage extension agents to exert greater effort. However, whether such interventions change agents' actual behavior and improve outcomes for farmers remains insufficiently understood.

This study uses a randomized controlled trial (RCT) to examine the causal effects of non-monetary interventions on the behavior of public agricultural extension agents (AEAs) and on farmer outcomes in Ghana. Specifically, we introduce two interventions: feedback from farmers and training that communicates the social value of extension work. The first intervention provides AEAs and their supervisors with regular feedback based on aggregate assessments by sampled farmers of the services provided by each AEA, without identifying individual respondents. This feedback may operate through several channels. By making farmers' assessments visible to AEAs and their supervisors, it may provide agents with a sense of recognition for their work. By reporting each agent's previous performance alongside district office averages and the highest average number of visits per farmer among peers, the feedback also facilitates performance comparisons over time and across agents \citep{Blanes2011, Ashraf2014}. Moreover, sharing the feedback with supervisors may strengthen supervisor monitoring. The second intervention consists of training that highlights the role of extension services in improving farmers' livelihoods and agricultural productivity. The training is designed not only to reinforce agents' recognition of the social significance of their work but also to strengthen their internal locus of control---that is, their belief that their own efforts and actions can make a meaningful difference to farmers' outcomes. Comparing the feedback-only group with the control group identifies the effect of feedback, while comparing the group receiving both feedback and training with the feedback-only group identifies the additional effect of training.

In addition, we cross-randomize a GPS stamp intervention with the two interventions described above. AEAs assigned to the GPS stamp are asked to record their locations when visiting farmers' fields using a smartphone-based form that they must activate themselves. Although recording is requested, compliance is not directly linked to rewards or penalties. This distinguishes our intervention from monitoring interventions examined in previous studies, which have often linked monitoring to financial incentives, such as recording teachers' attendance with photographs and linking it to their salaries \citep{Duflo2012}, or combining digital monitoring with performance-based pay \citep{Genicot2022}. The GPS stamp intervention is designed to examine the effects of GPS-based monitoring on AEAs' behavior, independently of the feedback and training interventions.

The experiment is conducted in all eight districts across four rain-fed rice-growing regions of Ghana---Oti, Bono East, Western North, and Eastern---covered by the Ghana Rice Production Improvement Project (GRIP) in the study period, implemented under the Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA) since 2022. We randomly assign the eight districts, and all 42 AEAs working in those districts, to three groups: three districts receive feedback from farmers only (T1); three receive both feedback and training on the social value of extension work (T2); and two serve as a control group (C), receiving neither intervention. Randomization is conducted at the district level because agents within the same district would closely exchange information with each other, creating a risk of spillovers if they are assigned to different groups. Separately, within each district, we randomly assign approximately half of the agents to the GPS stamp intervention, resulting in 20 of the 42 agents being assigned to this intervention. This ensures that agents with and without GPS stamps are represented in all three experimental groups. All agents in the three groups, including the control group, also received a small fuel allowance of 150 Ghana cedis per month (less than US\$15) through mobile money from April to August 2025. This allowance replaced similar support from a previous project that was phased out in early 2025, and was provided to keep agents’ operating conditions comparable between the baseline and endline periods. Because it was common to all groups, it does not affect the comparison across arms.

Feedback is provided to AEAs and their supervisors every two weeks from mid-April through September in 2025, the main rice-growing season. It is based on a biweekly phone survey of farmers conducted across all three groups, comprising 11 rounds over this period. Farmers associated with AEAs in the control group are surveyed in the same way, but the control-group AEAs and their supervisors do not receive feedback. The feedback reports each AEA's average number of visits per farmer, alongside their previous performance and that of other AEAs in the same district office. It also provides farmers' satisfaction ratings from 1 to 5 and their reasons for satisfaction or dissatisfaction with the extension services received. Baseline and endline surveys of AEAs, conducted in February to March 2025 and January to February 2026, respectively, measure their extension activities and psychological characteristics, including prosocial and intrinsic motivation as well as internal locus of control. We also randomly select approximately 400 households from the farmers served by the sample AEAs and collect data on satisfaction with extension services, knowledge and adoption of rice-farming technologies, rice productivity, and household income through baseline and endline surveys. The biweekly phone surveys provide repeated, farmer-reported measures of extension visits and other contacts, satisfaction with extension services, and the content of advice received, independently of the AEAs' own reports. Combining these data allows us to examine how non-monetary interventions and monitoring affect AEAs' psychological characteristics and behavior, as well as outcomes for farmers.

Three features of the design warrant comment. First, treatment was randomized across only eight districts. Because each region contains two study districts and our specifications include region fixed effects, each treatment--control comparison rests on very few within-region contrasts: T1 and the control group appear together only in Bono East, and T2 and the control group only in Oti. The 42 agents, although they include all AEAs serving rice-producing communities in these districts, are likewise too few for precise agent-level estimates. The 11 rounds of the biweekly farmer survey measure each agent's activities more precisely but do not add independent variation in treatment. We therefore report district-clustered standard errors together with wild cluster bootstrap and randomization-inference $p$-values, and we read our estimates as evidence from a small number of district comparisons rather than as precisely estimated population effects. Second, because control farmers were also surveyed regularly, control agents may have become aware that their visits were being recorded, which would bias the estimated effects toward zero. Third, all agents received a small fuel allowance during the intervention period, so our estimates capture the effects of non-monetary incentives in a setting where agents have limited support for transportation. Throughout, we treat the main effects of feedback, training, and GPS stamps as our primary analyses and leave their interactions for future work.

Our results yield four main findings. First, farmer feedback improved some dimensions of extension service delivery. During the planting season, farmers served by agents in T1 and T2 reported more visits, although the statistical significance of these estimates depends on the inference method. Over the full survey period, the increase in reported visits was confined to T2. Farmers in both treatment groups also reported greater satisfaction with extension services and were more likely to report receiving helpful advice. Second, the interventions did not affect agents’ intrinsic, extrinsic, or prosocial motivation but were associated with a stronger internal locus of control regarding agricultural production. Agents in the feedback-plus-training arm also scored higher on knowledge of recommended practices. Agents receiving feedback alone reported fewer visits, although this estimate is significant only at the 10 percent level. Third, we find no effects on rice yields or income in the single season covered by the endline survey. Farmers served by treated agents used less seed and herbicide, and the feedback-only arm reduced complete crop failure. Treated farmers also used more hired labor, mainly at harvest, and farmers in the feedback-plus-training arm used less family labor. Estimated profits rose when family labor is valued at the hired wage, but we treat this result as exploratory. In contrast, GPS stamp without associated rewards or penalties had no detectable effects on agents or farmers. Finally, agents’ self-reported visit measures differed substantially from farmer-reported visit frequencies and were not correlated with them. Moreover, farmer-reported visits declined with distance to the communities served, whereas agents’ self-reported measures did not exhibit the same pattern. These findings raise concerns about whether agent self-reports alone provide a reliable measure of service delivery. However, because the two measures are collected from different respondents and may capture different units or aspects of visiting activity, their differences should not be interpreted as direct evidence of systematic overreporting. 

This study makes three contributions. First, it contributes to the literature on agricultural extension in developing countries by focusing on the incentives faced by public extension agents. Existing experimental and non-experimental studies have primarily examined whether technical training improves farmers' technology adoption and productivity, and how the selection of training participants affects knowledge spillovers among farmers \citep[e.g.,][]{Kondylis2017, Ben2019, Beaman2021}. A related literature examines incentives for agricultural extension providers \citep{Takahashi2020}, including lead farmers \citep{Balew2023, Ben2019, Shikuku2019, Okello2023} and public extension agents. Among studies of the latter, \citet{Alam2024} compare monetary rewards with recognition from district directors in Bangladesh, and \citet{Genicot2022} examine pay-for-performance schemes linked to a digital monitoring system in Punjab, Pakistan. Our feedback intervention differs in that it conveys the assessments of service recipients rather than supervisors, and relies on neither monetary rewards nor formal performance-based incentives. By tracing the effects of this feedback, together with training and low-cost visit recording, from agents' activities to farmer outcomes, we provide evidence on whether such approaches can improve both the delivery and the effectiveness of public extension services. 

Second, this study contributes to the measurement of frontline public service delivery. Evaluating extension agents’ performance is difficult because administrative records and agents’ self-reports may not accurately reflect the services received by farmers. We address this measurement challenge by collecting biweekly reports directly from farmers in all experimental groups, independently of agents’ own reports. These data capture not only the frequency of visits and other contacts but also farmers’ satisfaction and the advice they receive. We document substantial discrepancies between agent-reported and farmer-reported visit measures, and find that the two measures are not correlated. We also find that farmer-reported visits, unlike agents’ self-reported visits, decline with distance to the communities served. These patterns highlight the value of incorporating service recipients’ reports when evaluating frontline service delivery, while also illustrating the need to interpret different measures of effort in light of how they are defined and collected.
%\textcolor{red}{For agents assigned to GPS stamps, we also obtain location-based visit records, which allows us to compare three measures based on different sources for this subsample: agents' self-reports, farmers' reports, and GPS records. Although GPS records are available only for agents whose behavior may itself respond to the intervention, this comparison enables us to assess the extent to which each measure captures agents' effort and deviations across measurements. Combining farmers' reports with location-based records submitted by extension agents may offer a low-cost approach to monitoring and evaluating extension services. Thus, this contribution has implications not only for research measurement, but also for the management and performance evaluation of public extension services.}

Finally, the study contributes to the broader literature on the motivation of public service providers. Studies of teachers and health workers have shown that incentives and beliefs about the impact of one's effort can influence service providers' behavior \citep{Duflo2012, Ashraf2014, Banuri2016, Mansuri2023, Kaur2023, Khan2025}. Non-monetary interventions are often presumed to work by changing providers' motivation or beliefs, yet relatively few studies measure these psychological characteristics directly alongside providers' behavior and beneficiaries' outcomes. We measure agents' intrinsic, extrinsic, and prosocial motivation and their internal locus of control before and after the interventions, and combine these measures with farmers' reports of agents' behavior and with farmer outcomes. This allows us to examine not only whether non-monetary interventions change providers' behavior, but also whether they do so by strengthening providers' motivation or by changing their beliefs about whether their own efforts make a difference to those they serve. Our findings provide suggestive evidence consistent with the possibility that non-monetary interventions change providers’ beliefs about whether their own efforts make a difference to those they serve. However, our design does not allow us to establish this mechanism causally.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides background on agricultural extension in Ghana. Section 3 describes the experimental design and data. Section 4 examines the measurement of extension-agent performance using farmer-reported data. Section 5 presents the estimated effects of the interventions on extension agents and farmers. Section 6 concludes.

\section{Agricultural extension in Ghana}\label{sec:context}

Agriculture plays a central role in Ghana's economy, employment, rural livelihoods, and food security. In 2025, agriculture accounted for 21.3\% of GDP and employed 34.6\% of the country's workforce \citep{worldbank_wdi_2026}. Agricultural production is dominated by smallholder farmers, many of whom rely on rain-fed farming and face constraints related to irrigation, access to improved technologies, and agricultural services \citet{gipc_agriculture_2025}. Improving agricultural productivity is therefore an important priority for economic development and poverty reduction.

Rice is one of Ghana's major staple foods, alongside maize, and its consumption has increased rapidly with population growth, urbanization, and changing dietary preferences. Despite the expansion of domestic production, Ghana remains heavily dependent on rice imports, with domestic production meeting only 55.1\% of national demand in 2024 \citep{ndpc_progress_2025}. Rice is cultivated under both irrigated and rain-fed conditions, predominantly by smallholder farmers. Many improved rice cultivation practices developed in Asia can be adapted to African conditions, suggesting substantial scope for productivity gains through their wider dissemination and adoption \citep{Otsuka2024}. Strengthening agricultural extension services is therefore an important component of efforts to increase domestic rice production and improve farmers' productivity and profitability. 

In Ghana, public extension services are coordinated by the Ministry of Food and Agriculture (MoFA) and delivered primarily through decentralized agricultural offices. According to \citet{MoFA2025Extension}, Ghana's national farmer-to-extension-agent ratio exceeds 1,500 farmers  per AEA, compared with a benchmark of 500 farmers per agent. During field visits to the study areas in early 2025, interviews with local agricultural officials and extension agents indicated that some agents were responsible for dozens of communities, serving farmers cultivating rice and other crops, but lacked sufficient funding for motorcycle fuel and maintenance. These constraints made it particularly difficult to visit more distant communities regularly. In addition to these resource constraints, weak incentives may limit agents' efforts to deliver advice and support farmers' adoption of improved practices \citep{Ragasa2013, Takahashi2020}. To strengthen rice extension, Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA) and MoFA are implementing the Ghana Rice Production Improvement Project (GRIP, 2022--2027), which provides technical training to extension agents and supports the dissemination of improved rice cultivation practices, including seed selection, land leveling, planting spacing, water management, and drying \citep{jica_grip_2022}. Building on these efforts to strengthen technical capacity, our study examines whether non-monetary incentives and monitoring can further improve extension agents' service delivery and outcomes for farmers.

\section{Experimental design and data}\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Experimental design}

In collaboration with JICA and the Institute of Statistical, Social and Economic Research (ISSER), University of Ghana, we conducted an RCT involving AEAs and associated farmers in rice-producing areas of Ghana. During the study period, the project covered ten sites under JICA's GRIP, comprising eight rain-fed rice-producing districts and two irrigation schemes. In this paper, we focus only on the rain-fed districts.
 
Each AEA serves multiple communities, including five to eight rice-producing areas. While their assigned communities generally do not overlap across different AEAs, they frequently exchange information with colleagues within the same district, which could generate spillovers if treatment were assigned at the AEA level. We therefore randomized treatment assignment at the district level, assigning the eight rain-fed districts to the following three groups: T1 (feedback only; three districts), T2 (feedback plus training; three districts), and a control group (two districts). In addition, within each district, we randomly selected approximately half of the AEAs (20 of 42 in total) to receive a cross-cutting GPS stamp intervention. Figure~\ref{fig:assignment} summarizes the experimental design, and Figure~\ref{fig:map} shows the study sites and their assigned treatment status.

During mid-April to the end of September 2025, which covers the main rice production season, we implemented the following interventions.
 
\textbf{Feedback}: The first intervention consisted of biweekly feedback reports based on farmers' assessments of their AEA's services. From the second half of April to the end of September 2025, we conducted a brief biweekly phone survey of sampled farmers in all three experimental groups. The questionnaire covered (1) extension activities---the number of visits by the AEA to the farmer or their community, and the number of successful contacts with the AEA by phone or in person, during the preceding two weeks; (2) satisfaction---farmers' satisfaction with the engagement during that period from 1 (not satisfactory at all) to 5 (very satisfactory); and (3) the reasons for their satisfaction or dissatisfaction. Because farmers in the control group were surveyed in the same way, comparisons between the treatment and control groups capture the effect of receiving feedback rather than the effect of being surveyed.
 
Based on the responses of the sampled farmers associated with each AEA (approximately ten farmers per AEA), we prepared a one-page PDF feedback report for each AEA in T1 and T2 and shared it with the AEA and their supervisor within two weeks. The report presented the AEA's average number of visits per farmer during the preceding two weeks, alongside the corresponding average from the previous survey round, the district average, and the highest average among AEAs in the district. It also reported aggregated satisfaction ratings and included farmers' stated reasons for satisfaction or dissatisfaction verbatim, without identifying individual respondents. Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:feedback} shows examples of the feedback reports. By communicating farmers' assessments to AEAs and their supervisors and enabling comparisons over time and across AEAs within the same district, the intervention was intended to encourage effort through performance feedback, social recognition, and accountability.
 
\textbf{Training}: We invited the 15 AEAs in T2 to an overnight sensitization training held on April 29, 2025 at a hotel in Koforidua, the capital of Ghana's Eastern Region. The training was delivered by two assistant directors of the Directorate of Agricultural Extension Services (DAES) of the Ministry of Food and Agriculture (MoFA). It did not provide technical instruction on rice production; instead, it emphasized the social value of agricultural extension and how AEAs' own efforts could improve farmers' livelihoods. All 15 invited AEAs attended. The training was intended to strengthen AEAs' recognition of the social significance of their work and their belief in their ability to make a difference through their own efforts. Similar interventions have been studied among community health workers in Pakistan \citep{Khan2025} and teachers in India \citep{Kaur2023}. Because the training was offered only in combination with feedback, the comparison between T2 and T1 captures the additional effect of training among AEAs receiving feedback.
 
\textbf{GPS stamp}: Within each of the eight districts, we randomly selected approximately half of the AEAs (20 of 42 in total) and asked them to record their GPS coordinates using a KoboToolbox form whenever they visited farmers. The procedure required only a smartphone; GPS coordinates were captured automatically when AEAs opened the form to record a visit. These records were collected for research purposes and were neither shared with supervisors nor linked to rewards or penalties. Nevertheless, the recording requirement could have increased AEAs' awareness that their activities were being monitored, thereby encouraging greater effort. Between mid-April and the end of September 2025, 19 of the 20 AEAs submitted at least one record, yielding a total of 357 records. Because these records exist only for AEAs assigned to the GPS stamp, they cannot be used as outcome measures for comparisons with other AEAs.
 
\textbf{Fuel support}: In addition to interventions above, we provided all 42 AEAs with a monthly fuel allowance of 150 Ghana cedis (less than US\$15 at the 2025 exchange rate) via mobile money between April and August 2025 to help cover the fuel costs of their motorbikes. AEAs were required to submit receipts for fuel purchases. A previous bilateral aid project had provided similar support to all district agricultural offices but was phased out in early 2025; we introduced this allowance to help maintain comparable operating conditions after that program ended. Because the allowance was provided to all AEAs regardless of treatment assignment, it does not confound comparisons across experimental groups. However, if the treatments and fuel support are complementary---for example, if AEAs can increase their visits only when fuel costs are covered---our estimates should be interpreted as treatment effects conditional on this support.

\subsection{Data}

In January and February 2025, prior to the baseline survey, we constructed the sampling frame in three steps.\footnote{
The sampling frame was constructed by a team led by
the ISSER, University of Ghana, with support from Japanese experts working on JICA's
GRIP.} First, we asked the directors of the eight District Departments of Agriculture to provide a complete list of AEAs whose assigned communities included rice-producing communities, along with the communities they served. This identified 42 AEAs. Second, we contacted each AEA and asked them to list all the rice-producing communities they served. This yielded 144 communities (Achiase: 11; Bibiani-Anhwiaso-Bekwai: 38; Biakoye: 19; Krachi West: 20; Lower Manya Krobo: 4; Pru West: 11; Sefwi Wiawso: 27; and Sene West: 14). Third, we asked each AEA to provide a list of eight randomly selected rice farmers from each rice-producing community they served. 

We selected farmers separately for each AEA. For AEAs serving multiple rice-producing communities, we randomly selected two communities and then randomly selected five farmers from the list of eight provided for each selected community, yielding ten sampled farmers per AEA. The remaining three farmers on each list were retained as replacements, and enumerators were instructed to interview a replacement if an initially selected farmer could not be interviewed. For AEAs serving only one rice-producing community, all eight farmers on the list were selected for the survey. If fewer than eight rice farmers were available in such communities, we interviewed all of them. Similarly, in communities selected from among those served by AEAs covering multiple communities, we interviewed all available rice farmers if fewer than five were available. This procedure yielded a target sample of 42 AEAs and 396 farmers.

The baseline and endline surveys, biweekly phone surveys, and interventions were implemented by a field research team.\footnote{The fieldwork and interventions were implemented by a team led by a professor at the Department of Agricultural Economics and Agribusiness, University of Ghana, with support from Japanese experts working on JICA's GRIP.} We monitored data quality through both on-site and remote checks. 

\textbf{Biweekly survey data}: To generate feedback reports and measure AEAs' extension activities, we conducted biweekly phone surveys of sample farmers in all three experimental groups from mid-April to the end of September 2025. These surveys provide measures of AEAs' extension-service performance from the perspective of the farmers they serve. Across 11 survey rounds, we collected 4,120 observations, representing more than 99\% of the targeted 4,158 farmer-round observations ($378 \times 11$).

\textbf{Baseline and endline survey data}: We evaluate the effects of the interventions on AEAs and farmers using the baseline survey, conducted from 18 February and 27 March 2025, and the endline survey, conducted between 12 January and 6 February 2026. \section{Measuring AEA performance}\label{sec:performance}

AEAs' efforts and performance are difficult for both supervisors and researchers to observe. Previous studies have relied on AEAs' self-reported efforts, such as the frequency of visits to their assigned communities \citep[e.g.,][]{Alam2024}, but such measures may be subject to reporting bias. As shown below, we find substantial discrepancies between AEAs' self-reported visits and farmers' reports, raising concerns about the reliability of self-reports. To measure extension-service delivery from the perspective of its recipients, we conducted biweekly phone surveys of sample farmers in all three experimental groups from mid-April to the end of September 2025 (eleven rounds in total). The survey results were used to prepare feedback reports, which were shared with AEAs and their supervisors in T1 and T2 but not in the control group. In this section, we first present descriptive results from the surveys and then examine differences in extension-service delivery across experimental groups using regression analysis.

\subsection{Descriptive results}

Figure~\ref{fig:bwbasic} shows, for each biweekly survey round, the average number of AEA visits per farmer, the number of contacts with AEAs (both remote and face-to-face), and farmers' satisfaction with extension services (on a scale from 1 to 5). In April, farmers in T1 and the control group received approximately 0.5 visits over two weeks, while those in T2 received more visits. Visit frequency increased from June and peaked in early July, coinciding with the planting season in our study areas, when farmers likely had greater demand for extension advice. AEAs may also have visited communities more frequently to disseminate rice production practices promoted by JICA's GRIP. Overall, farmers in T2 received more visits than those in T1 and the control group. The number of contacts followed a similar seasonal pattern, with control farmers reporting fewer contacts than farmers in T1 and T2 in most rounds. Average satisfaction with extension services generally increased over the survey period, although it fluctuated across rounds. Across the survey period, satisfaction was highest in T1, followed by T2 and the control group. Among those who answered that they were dissatisfied with their engagements with the AEA, roughly half of them (39 of 82, 47.6\%) cite the agent not visiting or not turning up as a reason of dissatisfaction.

\subsection{Regression analysis}

We estimate the intention-to-treat effects of the interventions on extension-service delivery as reported by sample farmers. Specifically, we estimate the following model by OLS:
\begin{equation}
Y_{ijdt} = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 T1_d + \alpha_2 T2_d + \alpha_3 GPS_j + \delta_t + \mu_r + \varepsilon_{ijdt},
\label{eq:bw}
\end{equation}
where $Y_{ijdt}$ denotes an outcome reported by farmer $i$, served by AEA $j$ in district $d$, in survey round $t$. $T1_d$ and $T2_d$ indicate assignment to the feedback-only and feedback-plus-training groups, respectively, while $GPS_j$ indicates assignment to the GPS stamp intervention. $\delta_t$ and $\mu_r$ denote survey-round and region fixed effects, respectively. The round fixed effects account for common seasonal variation in extension activities, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:bwbasic}. We cluster standard errors at the district level, the unit of randomization for T1 and T2. Given the small number of districts (eight), we also report $p$-values from the wild cluster bootstrap using Webb weights \citep{webb_reworking_2023} and from randomization inference, based on 1,000 reassignments of district-level treatment arms. 

Although we did not conduct a separate baseline survey for the biweekly outcomes, the first round of the phone survey was completed before the feedback reports were delivered and before the training took place. Our main specification includes all eleven survey rounds. As a robustness check, we exclude the first round from the estimation sample and control for each farmer's corresponding first-round outcome. The results remain similar. 

%We therefore interpret the results as correlations rather than causal effects.

Table~\ref{tab:bweffort} presents the estimated treatment effects on farmer-reported visits and contacts. Across all survey rounds, farmers in T2 reported 0.15 more visits per two weeks than those in the control group, an increase of approximately 29\% relative to the control mean of 0.51. The estimated effect of T1 is close to zero. During the planting season (June to the first half of July), both T1 and T2 farmers reported more visits than control farmers, with increases of 0.22 and 0.27 visits per two weeks, respectively. These estimates are statistically significant under randomization inference ($p=0.017$ for T1 and $p<0.001$ for T2), but not under the wild cluster bootstrap ($p=0.188$ and $0.107$, respectively). Similarly, estimate for T2 over the full survey period is significant under randomization inference ($p=0.022$) but not under the wild cluster bootstrap ($p=0.187$). We find no statistically significant effects on the number of contacts or from the GPS-recording intervention. Given the limited number of district-level clusters and the sensitivity of statistical significance to the inference method, we interpret the evidence of increased visits with caution.

\section{AEA- and farm-level impacts}\label{sec:impacts}

\subsection{Estimation model}

To estimate the impacts of the interventions on AEAs and farmers, we use the baseline and endline survey data. We employ an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) specification rather than a difference-in-differences (DID) specification because ANCOVA is generally more efficient when outcomes are weakly correlated over time \citep{mckenzie_beyond_2012}, as is often the case for farm income and profit. Controlling for baseline outcomes also helps account for the baseline differences across experimental groups documented in Section \ref{sec:design}. 

For the AEA-level analysis, we estimate
\begin{equation}
A_{jd1} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 T1_d + \beta_2 T2_d + \beta_3 GPS_j + \zeta A_{jd0} + \mu_r + \varepsilon_{jd},
\label{eq:ancova_aea}
\end{equation}
where $A_{jd1}$ and $A_{jd0}$ denote the outcome of AEA $j$ in district $d$ measured at endline and baseline, respectively.

For the farm-level analysis, we estimate
\begin{equation}
F_{ijd1} = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 T1_d + \gamma_2 T2_d + \gamma_3 GPS_j + \eta F_{ijd0} + \mu_r + \varepsilon_{ijd},
\label{eq:ancova_farm}
\end{equation}
where $F_{ijd1}$ and $F_{ijd0}$ denote the outcome of farmer $i$, served by AEA $j$ in district $d$, measured at endline (the 2025 agricultural season) and baseline (the 2024 agricultural season), respectively. 

In both equations, $T1_d$, $T2_d$ and $GPS_j$ are defined as in equation~(\ref{eq:bw}). As in Section~\ref{sec:performance}, standard errors are clustered at the district level, and we also report $p$-values from the wild cluster bootstrap and randomization inference. Because income and profit can take negative values, we use the inverse hyperbolic sine transformation for these outcomes. Coefficients on IHS-transformed outcomes cannot be interpreted as percentage changes when many observations are negative or near zero. A change in sign moves the transformed value by roughly ±9, so these coefficients largely reflect shifts in the share of farmers with positive income or profit. We therefore treat the level specifications as primary and additionally report the share of farmers with positive profit \textcolor{red}{do this later}.

\subsection{AEA-level impacts}
Panel A of Table \ref{tab:aeaimpact} presents the estimated impacts on AEAs' motivation and locus of control. We find no statistically significant effects on intrinsic, extrinsic, or prosocial motivation. In contrast, T1 increases general locus of control by 1.83 points, whereas T2 has no statistically significant effect. The difference between T1 and T2 is statistically significant ($p=0.002$). Both T1 and T2 have positive estimated effects on agriculture-specific locus of control (2.36 and 3.00 points, respectively), with no statistically significant difference ($p=0.255$). These positive estimates are statistically significant under conventional district-clustered inference and randomization inference, but not under the wild cluster bootstrap. Taken together, the findings suggest that the interventions may have strengthened AEAs' belief that their efforts can influence farmers' agricultural outcomes, rather than changing their underlying work motivation. 

Panel B presents the estimated impacts on AEAs' knowledge of rice production, participation in technical training, self-reported visits, and job satisfaction. T2 increases the knowledge score, measured using five questions on recommended rice production practices, by 0.92 points, whereas the estimated effect of T1 is small and statistically insignificant. The difference between T1 and T2 is statistically significant ($p=0.002$). Because the sensitization training did not provide technical instruction on rice production, this improvement is unlikely to reflect the training content itself. One possible explanation is that T2 increased AEAs' participation in technical training on rice production, including training provided under GRIP. T2 also increased job satisfaction by 0.69 points on a five-point scale, approximately 18\% of the control-group endline mean. The estimated effect of T1 is not statistically significant, and the difference between T1 and T2 is statistically significant at the 10\% level ($p=0.076$). T1 has a negative estimated effect on self-reported visit frequency, significant at the 10 percent level under conventional inference. However, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:performance}, self-reported visits are not a reliable measure of extension services received by farmers. We find no significant effect of the GPS stamp treatment. The positive T2 estimates for knowledge, training participation, and job satisfaction are statistically significant under randomization inference, but not at the 5\% level under the wild bootstrap.

Panel C explores the potential role of agriculture-specific locus of control by adding its endline value to the regression. Higher locus of control is positively associated with job satisfaction and negatively associated with self-reported visits, while its associations with knowledge and training participation are not statistically significant. Because locus of control is itself affected by treatment and may share unobserved determinants with these outcomes, this analysis does not identify causal mediation effects. We therefore interpret the results as exploratory rather than evidence that changes in locus of control caused the improvements in these outcomes.

\subsection{Farm-level impacts}

Table \ref{tab:farmimpact} presents the farm-level impacts. Neither T1 nor T2 has a statistically significant effect on rice yield (column 1 and 2, Panel A). Both, however, are associated with lower probability of crop failure, by 5.6 and 3.2 percentage points, respectively (column 3). We find no statistically significant effects on farm income (columns 4 and 5). In contrast, both treatments have positive estimated effects on farm profit, which accounts for the imputed cost of family labor. The estimated increases are GHS 2,651 per hectare for T1 and GHS 4,803 for T2 (column 6). Positive estimates are also obtained when the imputed costs of family labor devoted to bird scaring, the largest component of family labor, are excluded (column 8). %On average, they increased farm profit by 3.5\% and 3.7\%, respectively.

Evidence of reduced crop failure is less robust across inference methods. We find no statistically significant differences between T1 and T2 in their effects on crop failure or profit, and no statistically significant effects of the GPS stamp intervention.

To investigate the drivers of the positive impacts on farm profit, Panel B examines changes in input use. Both T1 and T2 reduced the quantity of seed applied, by 14.1 and 22.4 kg per hectare, respectively. At endline, control farmers used an average of 99 kg of seed per hectare, exceeding the recommended rate of 60--80 kg per hectare for direct seeding. Both treatments brought seed use closer to the recommended level (column 2). Both groups were also less likely to use herbicides and insecticides, while T2 increased machinery use by 13.7 percentage points. Some farmers in the biweekly survey reported receiving information from their AEAs about access to machinery. In addition, T1 and T2 increased hired labor use by 30.6 and 19.9 person-days per hectare, respectively, while T2 reduced family labor use by 50.7 person-days per hectare. Appendix Table \ref{tab:labour} further disaggregates these effects by farming activity and workers' gender. The decline in family labor under T2 was particularly pronounced in bird scaring, with additional reductions in land preparation and transplanting or sowing. The increase in hired labor in both treatment groups was driven primarily by harvesting and was concentrated among female workers. These changes in input and labor use may help explain the positive estimated effects on farm profit.

T1 increased the probability of using an improved rice variety by 28.0 percentage points, whereas T2 had no significant effect on this outcome. In contrast, T2 reduced the probability of transplanting rice in rows by 6.2 percentage points. Both estimates are statistically significant under randomization inference but not at the 5\% level under the wild cluster bootstrap. We find little evidence of effects on the adoption of other recommended practices. Appendix Table \ref{tab:prodtech} further examines the associations between these practices and farm outcomes. Both bund construction and the use of certified seed are positively associated with rice yield, while certified seed use is also associated with farm income and profit. These associations are observational and should be interpreted as causal effects of technology adoption.

Overall, the farm-level results point to changes in input use rather than in output. Both treatments reduced seed and herbicide use and increased hired labor; T2 also reduced family labor and increased machinery use. Because yields did not fall, these changes may reflect more efficient input use, possibly following AEAs’ advice, but the evidence for this link is indirect.

Taken together, the results are consistent with several possible channels through which the interventions may have affected service delivery and farm outcomes. The interventions changed some measures of agents’ internal locus of control and technical knowledge, while we find no clear effects on intrinsic, extrinsic, or prosocial motivation. Farmers in the treatment groups also reported higher satisfaction and were more likely to report receiving helpful advice; visit frequency increased during the planting period, although the estimates are sensitive to the inference method. These patterns suggest that feedback may have helped agents better understand farmers’ assessments of their services, while training may have reinforced their understanding of the social value of extension work. The observed changes in input and labor use are also consistent with farmers responding to extension advice. However, these findings do not identify a specific causal pathway linking agents’ psychological characteristics to their behavior and, in turn, to farm outcomes. We therefore interpret the proposed mechanisms as suggestive. We also acknowledge that several of the estimates underlying each link are statistically significant under randomization inference but not under the wild cluster bootstrap. The small number of clusters limits our statistical power, making it difficult to reveal the full picture of the intervention impacts.

First, the interventions strengthened AEAs' locus of control, that is, their belief that their own efforts can make a difference to farmers' outcomes. Second, the feedback reports told AEAs not only how often they visited their farmers but also why farmers were satisfied or dissatisfied with their service. This information may have made it clearer to AEAs what kind of service their farmers needed. Third, AEAs appear to have changed both the quantity and the quality of their service. Farmers in T2 reported more visits, and farmers in both treatment groups reported more visits during the planting season. At the same time, farmers in both treatment groups were more likely to be satisfied with their engagement with the AEA, even after controlling for the number of visits and contacts, and they were more likely to report that they had received helpful advice. Fourth, this advice, which mainly concerned day-to-day crop management such as fertilizer application, planting, and weeding, may have helped farmers use inputs more efficiently.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

In this study, we use a randomized controlled trial (RCT) to examine the causal effects of non-monetary interventions on the behavior of public agricultural extension agents (AEAs) in Ghana and on the outcomes of the farmers they serve. We introduced two non-monetary interventions for AEAs across eight districts: feedback reports based on biweekly phone surveys of farmers, and sensitization training on the importance of agricultural extension. We also introduced a cross-cutting GPS stamp treatment that required selected AEAs to submit records of their visits. Our biweekly surveys document that AEAs' self-reported visit frequencies are not correlated with farmer-reported visit frequencies, raising concerns about the reliability of self-reported efforts by extension agents. We also find suggestive evidence that both district-level treatments improved AEAs’ service delivery in terms of both quantity (the frequency of visits reported by farmers) and quality (farmers’ satisfaction). They were associated with a stronger internal locus of control in agricultural production, while they had no impact on intrinsic, extrinsic, or prosocial motivation. Agents in the feedback-plus-training arm also scored higher on knowledge of rice production and reported higher job satisfaction. For farmers, we find no effects on yields or income, but treated farmers used less seed and herbicide and more hired labor, mainly at harvest, and the feedback-only arm reduced complete crop failure. Profits rose when family labor is valued at the hired wage, although this result depends on how family labor is valued.

Our findings offer two policy implications. First, agricultural extension services require a better system to evaluate agents' efforts. In Ghana, AEAs are required to submit monthly reports to their district office directors, which include the number of visits to each community. During our field visits to several district offices, we found that in most monthly reports, AEAs reported visiting each of their communities eight or more times in the previous month, which is the frequency recommended by their supervising officers. However, we also observed that farmers complained that AEAs had not visited their communities as often as they were ``supposed to.'' Our biweekly phone survey data also show a substantial deviation between farmer-reported visit frequencies and AEAs' self-reported frequencies. Our phone-based survey offers one possible way to verify AEAs' self-reported efforts. Another approach is inspection by supervisors. In an experiment in Bangladesh, making the lowest-performing agents in each office subject to inspection improved their reported performance \citep{Alam2024}. 

Second, non-monetary incentives can improve agricultural extension service delivery. Previous studies show that training that strengthens mission motivation can improve the service delivery of community health workers \citep{Khan2025}, and that an intervention targeting teachers' beliefs about their control over student learning can increase their effort \citep{Kaur2023}. Our findings align with these studies in the context of agricultural extension. These results suggest that low-cost measures can improve agricultural extension service delivery in low-income countries, where monitoring and incentive are insufficient. 

Several limitations qualify these conclusions. First, treatment was randomized across only eight districts, limiting the precision of district-level treatment-effect estimates and making statistical inference sensitive to the method used. Second, farm-level characteristics and outcomes differed across groups at baseline, despite the randomized design. Although our specifications adjust for baseline outcomes and region fixed effects, residual uncertainty remains. Third, the study covers a single agricultural season, so the persistence of the effects and their implications for longer-term productivity and income remain unknown. Finally, because the feedback intervention combines farmer assessments, comparisons with other agents, and information shared with supervisors, the experiment does not separately identify the effects of each component. Future research could examine which elements of feedback are most effective, whether stronger accountability arrangements improve the effects of visit recording, and whether changes in service delivery translate into sustained improvements in farm productivity and welfare.

%Finally, we note three limitations of this study. First, the number of participating agents is modest, which limits the statistical power for agent-level outcomes, therefore, we provide only suggestive evidence. Second, we investigate only the short-term impacts of non-monetary interventions for AEAs. Their long-term impacts should be investigated to assess the cost-effectiveness of our interventions. Third, although we estimate the farm-level impacts, we do not fully capture the mechanisms through which the interventions for AEAs improved some of the farm-level outcomes. A better understanding of these mechanisms will help design more effective systems to improve extension service delivery. These three limitations provide avenues for future research.
