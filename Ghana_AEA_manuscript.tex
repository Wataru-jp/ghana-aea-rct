\section{Experimental design and data}\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Experimental design}

In collaboration with Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA) and Institute of Statistical, Social and Economic Research (ISSER), University of Ghana, we conducted a randomized controlled trial (RCT) with agricultural extension agents (AEAs) in rice producing sites in Ghana. These ten areas are target sites of JICA's Ghana Rice Production Improvement Project (GRIP). This includes eight rainfed rice-producing districts and two irrigation schemes.

We randomized at the district level. AEAs within the same district are each responsible for several communities (five to eight in our sample), and their assigned communities usually do not overlap. Nevertheless, they all report to the same district director and commute to the same district office, so randomization at the AEA level could cause the intervention effects to spill over to AEAs in the control group. We randomly assigned the eight rainfed districts to T1 (three districts), T2 (three districts), and the control group (two districts). In addition, we introduced a cross-cutting GPS treatment (described below) to a randomly selected half of the AEAs in each district. Because the production environment of the two irrigation schemes differs substantially from that of the rainfed areas, the schemes were excluded from the randomization and received no treatment. Although we also surveyed the irrigation schemes, we do not use these data, and the remainder of this paper covers only the rainfed areas. Figure~\ref{fig:map} presents the study sites and their assigned treatment status.

Following the baseline survey, we introduced three treatments for the corresponding AEAs from mid-April to the end of September 2025, a period that covers the rice production season.

\textbf{Feedback}: The first intervention is a bi-weekly feedback report based on farmers' evaluations of their AEA's service. From the second half of April to the end of September 2025, we conducted a brief bi-weekly phone survey of all sample farmers. The questionnaire covered (1) effort---whether the AEA had visited the community or the farmer in the past two weeks and, if so, how many times; (2) satisfaction---how satisfied the farmer was with the AEA's service in the past two weeks; and (3) the reason for that satisfaction or dissatisfaction. Based on the responses of each AEA's sample farmers (around ten farmers per AEA), we produced a one-page report in PDF format for AEAs in T1 and T2 only and shared it with each AEA within two weeks. Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:feedback} shows examples of the feedback report. This treatment aims to enhance AEAs' intrinsic motivation through social recognition.

\textbf{Training}: We invited the 15 AEAs in T2 to an overnight sensitization training, held in April 2025 at a hotel in Koforidua, the capital of the Eastern Region of Ghana. The training was delivered by two assistant directors of the Directorate of Agricultural Extension Services (DAES) of the Ministry of Food and Agriculture (MoFA). It contained no technical training on rice production; instead, it focused on the social impacts and importance of agricultural extension. All 15 invited AEAs attended. This treatment aims to strengthen AEAs' locus of control over their extension services. Similar interventions have been shown to be effective among community health workers in Pakistan \citep{khan_mission_2025} and teachers in India \citep{kaur_how_nodate}.

\textbf{GPS stamp}: We asked 20 randomly selected AEAs across the eight districts to submit their GPS coordinates through a KoboToolbox form whenever they visited farmers. This ``light'' monitoring treatment requires no special device other than a smartphone, and AEAs cannot manipulate the recorded location. Between mid-April and the end of September, 19 of the 20 AEAs submitted at least one record, and 357 records were submitted in total.

\textbf{Fuel support}: Between April and August 2025, we provided 150 Ghana cedis (less than US\$15 at the 2025 exchange rate) through mobile money to all AEAs. This support was given to both the treatment and control arms to cover fuel for their motorbikes. A former bilateral project had provided similar support to all district offices in previous years, but it was phased out in early 2025. We introduced comparable support for the AEAs in our sample district offices to minimize changes in the extension environment between the baseline and endline periods.

\subsection{Data}

In January and February 2025, ahead of the baseline survey, we compiled a complete list of AEAs and farmers in three steps. First, we obtained a full list of AEAs and the communities in their charge from the directors of the eight District Departments of Agriculture. This identified 42 AEAs. Second, we called each AEA and asked them to list all the rice-producing communities they serve, which identified 144 communities (Achiase 11; Bibiani-Anhwiaso-Bekwai 38; Biakoye 19; Krachi West 20; Lower Manya Krobo 4; Pru West 11; Sefwi Wiawso 27; and Sene West 14). Last, we asked the AEAs to list eight randomly selected rice farmers in each community.

We sampled farmers at the AEA level. For each AEA, two communities were randomly selected, and five farmers were randomly selected from each of them. The remaining three farmers on each list were kept as reserves, and enumerators were instructed to interview a reserve farmer when any of the five selected farmers could not be interviewed. In communities with fewer than five rice farmers, we interviewed all of them. This resulted in a target sample of 42 AEAs and 396 farmers.

The implementation of the surveys and the interventions was commissioned to ISSER. Throughout the process, we monitored and controlled data quality both on site and online. Between January 2025 and January 2026, we conducted and collected data from baseline survey, bi-weekly phone survey as a part of the interventions, and endline survey.

\textbf{Bi-weekly survey data}: As part of the feedback intervention, we conducted the bi-weekly phone survey from mid-April to the end of September 2025. These data measure the AEAs' performance in extension service from the perspective of its recipients. Over 11 rounds, we collected 4,120 observations, more than 99\% of the target ($378 \times 11 = 4{,}158$ observations).

\textbf{Baseline and endline survey data}: We evaluate the AEA- and farm-level impacts of the interventions using the baseline survey, conducted between 18 February and 27 March 2025, and the endline survey, conducted between 12 January and 6 February 2026. Table~\ref{tab:obs} compares the target and actual sample sizes. We interviewed all 42 AEAs at baseline and 41 at endline, because one AEA retired before the endline survey. For farmers, we interviewed 378 at baseline, and 373 of them again at endline (an attrition rate of 1.3\%).

The AEA questionnaire covered individual characteristics and career history (age, gender, education, civil-service grade, and tenure), prosocialness, work motivation (intrinsic and extrinsic), locus of control over agricultural production and their own career as well as in general, technical knowledge of recommended rice cultivation practices, self-reported extension effort (the number of farmer groups served and the frequency of visits), job satisfaction, and participation in training. Farmers were asked about household demographics and assets, rice production in the 2024 season on their representative plot (area, output, and sales), input use and costs, family and hired labor by farm operation, the adoption of recommended management practices, land tenure, and their contact with and satisfaction with their AEA. Apart from minor modifications, the endline questionnaires were identical to those of the baseline but asked about performance in the 2025 season.

\subsection{Descriptive statistics}

Table~\ref{tab:descaea} shows descriptive statistics of the surveyed AEAs. There is no significant difference between treatment arms in their basic characteristics (Panel A). On average, they assist six to eight farmer groups. We also measured the AEAs' prosocialness, motivation, and locus of control, and found no significant difference at baseline; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:scales} gives the details of these measurements. Regarding knowledge of rice production, there is no significant difference across treatment arms on the 0--5 scale. Both knowledge indices are built from the AEAs' answers to five questions on recommended practices: seed selection, land levelling, planting spacing, water management, and drying. The 0--5 index gives one point for each correct answer, and the seed-selection question, which allows multiple answers, earns its point only when the AEA names all four valid purposes of seed selection. The 0--8 index instead counts how many of the four purposes the AEA names (0--4) and adds the other four items as they are. When the score is stretched from the 0--5 to the 0--8 scale in this way, a significant difference appears between T1 and the control group (4.25 against 3.10). Nonetheless, there is no difference in the other variables, including training experience, self-reported average monthly visits to farmer groups, and job satisfaction. Overall, there are no systematic differences in AEA characteristics between the treatment and control groups.

Table~\ref{tab:descfarmer} shows descriptive statistics of the surveyed farmers. A typical sample household is headed by a man in his late forties and has six to seven members, and it cultivates a rice plot of about two hectares. At baseline, average yields in the 2024 season were only 0.9 to 1.3 tons per hectare, and 13.5\% of control households lost their crop entirely. About 80\% of farmers applied herbicides or insecticides, while chemical fertilizer was used by only half to two-thirds of them. Machinery, mostly tractors and power tillers for land preparation, was used by 73\% of control farmers but by only 38\% in T1 and 50\% in T2. On average, farmers earned a positive income from rice, between 1,400 and 4,600 GHS per hectare, but once family labor is valued at the local hired wage, profit becomes negative in all three arms. This is because rice farming in our sample relies heavily on family labor, a large part of which is spent on bird scaring.

Unlike the AEAs, farmers in the three arms differ in some areas at baseline. Compared with the control group, T1 farmers used two to three times as much hired and family labor per hectare and earned a higher income, and both treatment groups experienced crop failure less often. Dibbling was much more common in T1 (54\% against 5\% in the control group), and treated farmers more often planted improved varieties. Treated farmers were also less likely to have had any contact with their AEA in the 2024 season (67\% in T1 and 77\% in T2 against 92\%), although those who had contact were somewhat more satisfied with the service. These differences reflect the fact that we randomized only eight districts, so that differences in the production environment across districts are not fully balanced out. To account for this, we control for the baseline value of each outcome and for region fixed effects in the impact analysis (Section~\ref{sec:impacts}).

\section{Measuring AEA performance}\label{sec:performance}

AEAs' efforts and performance are hard to observe for both supervisors and researchers. While some studies rely on AEAs' self-reported efforts, such as the number of visits to their assigned communities, we show later that AEAs tend to overstate their effort levels in the absence of an effective monitoring system. In this study, we conducted a phone survey of all sample farmers, the direct recipients of extension services, every two weeks between mid-April and the end of September 2025 (eleven rounds in total). During the intervention period, feedback reports were produced from the survey results and shared with each AEA in the treatment groups (both T1 and T2). In this section, we first present descriptive results of the surveys and then compare the control and treatment groups with regression analysis.

\subsection{Descriptive results}

Figure~\ref{fig:bwbasic} shows, for each round of the survey, the average number of AEA visits to farmers, the number of contacts with farmers (both online and face-to-face), and the level of satisfaction with the service (1 = lowest, 5 = highest). In April, the average number of visits in two weeks was around 0.5 per farmer in T1 and in the control group, while it was higher in T2. The number of visits rises from June and peaks in early July. This period corresponds to planting and early crop establishment in our study areas, when farmers presumably need advice the most. AEAs may also have visited communities more often to disseminate the production technologies they learnt as part of the project. Overall, T2 farmers received more visits than T1 and control farmers. The number of contacts follows a similar trend. One key difference is that control farmers had fewer contacts with their AEA than T1 and T2 farmers did in most rounds. The average level of satisfaction improved over the survey period in all groups. On average, it was highest among T1 farmers, followed by T2 farmers and then control farmers.

Figure~\ref{fig:selfrep} compares, for each AEA, the number of visits reported by farmers with the number of visits the AEA reported in the baseline and endline surveys. Both are converted to a monthly basis: visits per farmer group for the AEA's self-report and visits per farmer for the farmers' reports. On average, AEAs reported that they visited each group nine times a month at baseline and thirteen times at endline. These numbers exceed the visiting frequency recommended by the district offices, which is to visit every community at least eight times a month. However, farmers reported only around 1.5 visits per month on average, and almost all AEAs lie below the 45-degree line, which implies that AEAs overstate their efforts. More importantly, there is no correlation between the two measures, self-reported and farmer-reported visits.

Figure~\ref{fig:distvisits} further presents the relationship between the number of visits and the distance from the AEA's duty station (district office) to the assigned communities, averaged over the communities. On average, the assigned communities are 5.4 km away from the district office. The results are in contrast. Self-reported visits show no relation with distance. On the other hand, farmer-reported visits show a clear negative correlation with distance. This result is plausible given the higher transportation costs to more remote communities, and it suggests that the farmer-reported visits collected through the bi-weekly surveys reflect the actual effort levels of AEAs.
