\section{Introduction}

Addressing global food insecurity and poverty, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa, requires sustained improvements in agricultural productivity. Such improvements depend not only on the development of agricultural technologies but also on their effective dissemination and adoption by farmers. Public agricultural extension services play a central role in this process, as knowledge spillovers among farmers can lead to the under provision of privater extension services \citep{Takahashi2020}. Yet public extension services in developing countries often suffer from weak service delivery, including agent absenteeism and infrequent visits to farmers \citep{Anderson2007, Alam2024}. Even when extension agents possess relevant technical knowledge and operational resources, the effectiveness of their services depends on the effort they exert in reaching farmers and supporting technology adoption. This raises a central question: How can extension agents be motivated to deliver more effective services?

This question is related to the incentive problem common to public service delivery in developing countries. Absenteeism among teachers and health workers is widespread \citep{Chaudhury2006}, and even when present, providers often fail to deliver services commensurate with their knowledge \citep{Das2007, Finan2017}. Because providers’ performance is hard to measure and monitoring systems are weak in many developing countries, individual effort is only loosely linked to rewards: working harder often brings neither higher pay nor better promotion prospects, which weakens providers’ incentives to perform well. Research has shown that incentive schemes involving monetary rewards, monitoring, and public recognition can improve service providers' behavior \citep{Muralidharan2011, Duflo2012, Banerjee2008, Ashraf2014, Leaver2021, Mansuri2023}.  
 %and that compensation and prosocial motivation shape who enters public service \citep{Bo2013, Banuri2016}. 
 In addition, while providers may exert less effort when they believe that their work is unlikely to improve the lives of the people they serve \citep{Kaur2023}, interventions that remind service providers of the mission of their work have been found to increase effort \citep{Khan2025}. Similar challenges arise in agricultural extension. \citet{Ragasa2013} reports that a lack of encouragement from supervisors undermines extension agents' motivation, whereas expressions of gratitude from farmers increase it. These findings suggest that non-monetary interventions that communicate farmers' evaluations or the social value of extension work may strengthen extension agents' motivation. However, whether such interventions change agents' actual behavior and improve outcomes for farmers remains insufficiently understood.

This study uses a randomized controlled trial (RCT) to examine the causal effects of non-monetary interventions on the behavior of public agricultural extension agents (AEAs) and on farmer outcomes in Ghana. Specifically, we introduce two interventions: feedback from farmers and training that communicates the social value of extension work. The first intervention provides AEAs and their supervisors with regular feedback on farmers' assessments of extension services. This feedback can operate through several channels. By making farmers' assessments visible, it may increase the social recognition agents receive from farmers and supervisors. Because it also reports the average for the agent's district office and the figures for the highest-rated agent in that office, it introduces an element of social comparison with peers \citep{Blanes2011, Ashraf2014}. Also, because it is shared with supervisors, it may function as a simple form of supervisory monitoring. The second intervention is training that explains the role extension services play in improving farmers' livelihoods and agricultural productivity. This intervention is intended not only to help extension agents recognize the social significance of their work but also to strengthen their internal locus of control and self-efficacy---that is, their belief that their own efforts and actions can make a meaningful difference to farmers' outcomes. Comparing the feedback-only group with the control group identifies the effect of feedback, while comparing the group receiving both feedback and training with the feedback-only group identifies the additional effect of emphasizing the social value of extension work and agents' capacity to influence farmer outcomes.

In addition, we cross-randomize a GPS-stamp intervention with the two interventions above, in which extension agents record their location when visiting farmers' fields. This is a simple monitoring tool that makes agents' effort more visible by creating a location-based record of visits. Monitoring interventions examined in previous studies have often been external mechanisms tied to rewards or penalties, such as recording teachers' attendance with photographs and linking it to their salaries \citep{Duflo2012}, or combining digital monitoring with performance-based pay \citep{Genicot2022}. Within each district, we randomly assign GPS stamps, which are not linked to rewards, to half of the agents, rounded down (20 of the 42 agents in total), so that agents with and without GPS stamps are present in all three groups. This design allows us to compare the effects of feedback and training on the social value of extension work with those of simple monitoring. \textcolor{red}{It also enables us to test whether monitoring complements or substitutes for feedback in motivating extension agents.}

The experiment is conducted in eight districts across four rain-fed rice-growing regions of Ghana---Oti, Bono East, Western North, and Eastern---covered by the Ghana Rice Production Improvement Project (GRIP), implemented by the Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA) since 2022. We randomly assign the eight districts, and thus the 42 participating AEAs working in them, to three groups: three districts receive feedback from farmers; three receive feedback as well as training on the social value of extension work; and two serve as a control group and receive neither intervention. We randomize at the district level because agents in the same district report to the same district director and work from the same office, so assigning them to different groups could lead to spillovers. In addition, half of the agents within each district (20 of the 42) are randomly selected to receive the GPS-stamp intervention. All agents in the three groups, including the control group, also received a small fuel allowance of 150 Ghana cedis per month (less than US\$15) through mobile money from April to August 2025. This allowance replaced similar support from a previous project that was phased out in early 2025, and was provided to keep agents’ operating conditions comparable between the baseline and endline periods. Because it was common to all groups, it does not affect the comparison across arms.

Feedback is provided to AEAs and their supervisors every two weeks from April through September in 2025, the main period of rice cultivation. It draws on a biweekly phone-based survey of farmers conducted in all three groups, for a total of 11 rounds over the same period; agents in the control group are surveyed in the same way but do not receive the feedback results. The feedback reflects each agent's number of farmer visits and farmer satisfaction alongside the average for the relevant district office and the corresponding scores for the highest-rated agent in that office. Baseline and endline surveys of AEAs, implemented in February 2025 and January 2026, respectively, measure their extension activities and psychological characteristics, including prosocial and intrinsic motivation. We also randomly select approximately 400 households from the farmers associated with the sample AEAs and collect data on satisfaction with extension services, knowledge and adoption of rice-farming technologies, rice productivity, and household income in baseline and endline surveys. Through the high-frequency surveys implemented in-between, we continuously measure these farmers' reports of the frequency of extension agents' visits and other contact, their satisfaction, and the content of advice received, independently of the agents' own reports. Combining these data allows us to examine how non-monetary interventions and monitoring affect agents' psychological characteristics and behavior, as well as outcomes for farmers.

Three features of the design warrant comment. First, the number of agents is modest, which limits statistical power for agent-level outcomes and for comparisons across the six cells of the cross-randomized design. The biweekly farmer survey partly mitigates this concern by providing repeated observations of each agent's activities over 11 rounds, which improves the precision of our estimates. Given the small number of agents and clustered, we report standard errors clustered at the district level together with wild cluster bootstrap and randomization-inference $p$-values. We treat the main effects of feedback, training, and GPS stamps as our primary analyses, \textcolor{red}{and the interaction between feedback and GPS stamps and the analysis of mechanisms as exploratory}. Second, because farmers in the control group are also surveyed regularly, control agents may become aware that their visits are being recorded. Such monitoring effect in the control group would bias our estimates of the treatment effects downward, so the resultant impacts, especially from social recognition, would be interpreted as lower bounds on the true effects. Third, because all agents received a small fuel allowance during the intervention period, our estimates should be interpreted as the effects of non-monetary incentives when agents have minimal resources for transportation.

This study makes three contributions. First, it contributes to the literature on agricultural extension in developing countries by shifting the focus from farmers to the public agents responsible for delivering extension services, and in particular to the incentives these agents face. Existing experimental and non-experimental studies have focused primarily on two questions: whether technical training improves farmers' technology adoption and productivity, and how to select training participants to facilitate knowledge spillovers among farmers \citep[e.g.,][]{Kondylis2017, Ben2019, Beaman2021}. A related literature examines incentives for agricultural extension providers \citep{Takahashi2020}, including lead farmers \citep{Balew2023, Ben2019, Shikuku2019, Okello2023} and public extension agents \citep{Alam2024, Genicot2022}. Among studies focusing on public extension agents, \citet{Alam2024} compare monetary rewards with recognition from district directors in Bangladesh, while \citet{Genicot2022} examine pay-for-performance schemes linked to a digital monitoring system in Punjab, Pakistan. We complement this literature by experimentally evaluating two non-monetary interventions---feedback on farmers' assessments of extension services and training that emphasizes the social value of extension work. These interventions target agents' responsiveness to farmers' evaluations and their motivation to improve farmers' livelihoods, rather than relying primarily on monetary rewards or formal performance-based incentives. The crossed GPS-stamp intervention further allows us to evaluate the effects of simpler and lower-cost monitoring alongside these non-monetary interventions. By examining the effects of these interventions on both agents' activities and farmer outcomes, we provide evidence on whether non-monetary approaches can improve the delivery and effectiveness of public agricultural extension services.

Second, the study contributes to the measurement of extension-agent performance. Agents' effort is difficult to observe directly, and previous studies have measured extension activities mainly using such information as self-reports and administrative records. Self-reports create incentives for overreporting, raising particular concerns about reliability. Administrative records can capture the number of visits, but are less able to measure the content of advice or its usefulness to farmers. In this study, we conduct high-frequency surveys of farmers in all groups every two weeks during the intervention period. These surveys measure, at the farmer level and independently of agents' own reports, the frequency of agents' visits and other contact, farmer satisfaction, and the content of advice. \textcolor{red}{For agents assigned to GPS stamps, we also obtain location-based visit records, which allows us to compare three measures based on different sources for this subsample: agents' self-reports, farmers' reports, and GPS records. Although GPS records are available only for agents whose behavior may itself respond to the intervention, this comparison enables us to assess the extent to which each measure captures agents' effort and deviations across measurements. A system that continuously measures extension-agent performance at the farmer level may itself serve as a low-cost tool for monitoring and evaluation. Thus, this contribution has implications not only for research measurement, but also for the management and performance evaluation of public extension services.}


Finally, the study contributes to the broader literature on the motivation of public service providers. Studies of teachers and health workers have shown that incentives and beliefs about the impact of one's effort can influence service providers' behavior \citep{Duflo2012, Ashraf2014, Banuri2016, Mansuri2023, Kaur2023, Khan2025}. Non-monetary interventions are often presumed to work by changing providers' motivation or beliefs, yet few studies measure these psychological characteristics directly alongside providers' behavior and beneficiaries' outcomes. We measure agents' psychological characteristics, including prosocial and intrinsic motivation, before and after the interventions, and combine these measures with farmers' reports of agents' behavior and farmer outcomes. We find that feedback combined with training increased agents' visits and farmer satisfaction, whereas we find little evidence that either intervention changed agents' self-reported psychological characteristics. Although the limited number of agents and the timing of the endline survey, conducted several months after the interventions ended, call for caution in interpreting these null results, this pattern suggests that the training may have worked less by durably reshaping agents' motivation than by helping them translate farmers' feedback into action. The findings have policy implications for governments in developing countries and development agencies seeking to improve the effectiveness of agricultural extension services under tight budget constraints.

\section{Agricultural extension in Ghana}\label{sec:context}

Agriculture plays a central role in Ghana's economy, employment, rural livelihoods, and food security. In 2025, agriculture
accounted for 21.3\% of GDP and employed 34.6\% of the country's workforce \citep{worldbank_wdi_2026}. Agricultural production is dominated by smallholder farmers, many of whom rely on rain-fed farming and face constraints
related to irrigation, access to improved technologies, and agricultural services \citep{gipc_agriculture_2025}.
Improving agricultural productivity is therefore an important priority for economic development and poverty reduction.

Rice is one of Ghana's major staple foods, alongside maize, and its consumption has increased rapidly with population
growth, urbanization, and changing dietary preferences. Despite the expansion of domestic production, Ghana remains
heavily dependent on rice imports, with domestic production meeting only 55.1\% of national demand in 2024
\citep{ndpc_progress_2025}. Rice is cultivated under both irrigated and rain-fed conditions, predominantly by smallholder farmers. Many improved rice cultivation practices developed in Asia can be adapted to African conditions, suggesting substantial scope for productivity gains through their wider dissemination and adoption \citep{Otsuka2024}. Strengthening agricultural extension services is therefore an important component of efforts to increase domestic rice production and improve farmers' productivity and profitability. 

In Ghana, public extension services are coordinated by the Ministry of Food and Agriculture (MoFA) and develivered primarily through decentralized agricultural offices. Their effectiveness depends not only on extension agents' technical knowledge and operational resources but also on their effort and incentives to engage with farmers. According to \citep{MoFA2025Extension}, each agricultural extension agent (AEA) serves more than 1,500 farmers, compared with a benchmark of 500 farmers per agent. During field visits to the study areas in early 2025, interviews with local agricultural officials and extension agents indicated that some agents were responsible for dozens of communities, serving farmers cultivating rice and other crops, but lacked sufficient funding for motorcycle fuel and maintenance. These constraints made it particularly difficult to visit more distant communities regularly. Moreover, even when agents possess relevant technical knowledge, weak incentives may limit their efforts to deliver advice and support farmers' adoption of improved practices \citep{Ragasa2013, Takahashi2020}. To strengthen rice extension, JICA and MoFA are implementing the Ghana Rice Production Improvement Project (GRIP, 2022--2027), which provides technical training to extension agents and supports the dissemination of improved rice cultivation practices, including water management, harvesting, and post-harvest handling \citep{jica_grip_2022}. Building on these efforts to strengthen technical capacity, our study examines whether non-monetary incentives and monitoring can further improve extension agents' service delivery and outcomes for farmers.

\section{Experimental design and data}\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Experimental design}

In collaboration with Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA) and Institute of Statistical, Social and Economic Research (ISSER), University of Ghana, we conducted a randomized controlled trial (RCT) with agricultural extension agents (AEAs) in rice producing sites in Ghana. These ten areas are target sites of JICA's Ghana Rice Production Improvement Project (GRIP). This includes eight rainfed rice-producing districts and two irrigation schemes.

We randomized at the district level. AEAs within the same district are each responsible for several rice-producing communities (five to eight in our sample), and their assigned communities usually do not overlap. Nevertheless, they all report to the same district director and commute to the same district office, so randomization at the AEA level could cause the intervention effects to spill over to AEAs in the control group. We randomly assigned the eight rainfed districts to T1 (three districts), T2 (three districts), and the control group (two districts). In addition, we introduced a cross-cutting GPS treatment (described below) to a randomly selected half of the AEAs in each district. Because the production environment of the two irrigation schemes differs substantially from that of the rainfed areas, the schemes were excluded from the randomization and received no treatment. Although we also surveyed the irrigation schemes, we do not use these data, and the remainder of this paper covers only the rainfed areas. Figure~\ref{fig:map} presents the study sites and their assigned treatment status.

Following the baseline survey, we introduced three treatments for the corresponding AEAs from mid-April to the end of September 2025, a period that covers the rice production season.

\textbf{Feedback}: The first intervention is a bi-weekly feedback report based on farmers' evaluations of their AEA's service. From the second half of April to the end of September 2025, we conducted a brief bi-weekly phone survey of all sample farmers. The questionnaire covered (1) effort---whether the AEA had visited the community or the farmer in the past two weeks and, if so, how many times; (2) satisfaction---how satisfied the farmer was with the AEA's service in the past two weeks; and (3) the reason for that satisfaction or dissatisfaction. Based on the responses of each AEA's sample farmers (around ten farmers per AEA), we produced a one-page report in PDF format for AEAs in T1 and T2 only and shared it with each AEA within two weeks. Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:feedback} shows examples of the feedback report. This treatment aims to enhance AEAs' intrinsic motivation through social recognition.

\textbf{Training}: We invited the 15 AEAs in T2 to an overnight sensitization training, held in April 2025 at a hotel in Koforidua, the capital of the Eastern Region of Ghana. The training was delivered by two assistant directors of the Directorate of Agricultural Extension Services (DAES) of the Ministry of Food and Agriculture (MoFA). It contained no technical training on rice production; instead, it focused on the social impacts and importance of agricultural extension. All 15 invited AEAs attended. This treatment aims to strengthen AEAs' locus of control over their extension services. Similar interventions have been shown to be effective among community health workers in Pakistan \citep{khan_mission_2025} and teachers in India \citep{kaur_how_nodate}.

\textbf{GPS stamp}: We asked 20 randomly selected AEAs across the eight districts to submit their GPS coordinates through a KoboToolbox form whenever they visited farmers. This ``light'' monitoring treatment requires no special device other than a smartphone, and AEAs cannot manipulate the recorded location. Between mid-April and the end of September, 19 of the 20 AEAs submitted at least one record, and 357 records were submitted in total.

\textbf{Fuel support}: Between April and August 2025, we provided 150 Ghana cedis (less than US\$15 at the 2025 exchange rate) through mobile money to all AEAs. This support was given to both the treatment and control arms to cover fuel for their motorbikes. A former bilateral project had provided similar support to all district offices in previous years, but it was phased out in early 2025. We introduced comparable support for the AEAs in our sample district offices to minimize changes in the extension environment between the baseline and endline periods.

\subsection{Data}

In January and February 2025, ahead of the baseline survey, we compiled a complete list of AEAs and farmers in three steps. First, we obtained a full list of AEAs and the communities in their charge from the directors of the eight District Departments of Agriculture. This identified 42 AEAs. Second, we called each AEA and asked them to list all the rice-producing communities they serve, which identified 144 communities (Achiase 11; Bibiani-Anhwiaso-Bekwai 38; Biakoye 19; Krachi West 20; Lower Manya Krobo 4; Pru West 11; Sefwi Wiawso 27; and Sene West 14). Last, we asked the AEAs to list eight randomly selected rice farmers in each community.

We sampled farmers at the AEA level. For each AEA, two communities were randomly selected, and five farmers were randomly selected from each of them. The remaining three farmers on each list were kept as reserves, and enumerators were instructed to interview a reserve farmer when any of the five selected farmers could not be interviewed. In communities with fewer than five rice farmers, we interviewed all of them. This resulted in a target sample of 42 AEAs and 396 farmers.

The implementation of the surveys and the interventions was commissioned to ISSER. Throughout the process, we monitored and controlled data quality both on site and online. Between January 2025 and January 2026, we conducted and collected data from baseline survey, bi-weekly phone survey as a part of the interventions, and endline survey.

\textbf{Bi-weekly survey data}: As part of the feedback intervention, we conducted the bi-weekly phone survey from mid-April to the end of September 2025. These data measure the AEAs' performance in extension service from the perspective of its recipients. Over 11 rounds, we collected 4,120 observations, more than 99\% of the target ($378 \times 11 = 4{,}158$ observations).

\textbf{Baseline and endline survey data}: We evaluate the AEA- and farm-level impacts of the interventions using the baseline survey, conducted between 18 February and 27 March 2025, and the endline survey, conducted between 12 January and 6 February 2026. Table~\ref{tab:obs} compares the target and actual sample sizes. We interviewed all 42 AEAs at baseline and 41 at endline, because one AEA retired before the endline survey. For farmers, we interviewed 378 at baseline, and 373 of them again at endline (an attrition rate of 1.3\%).

\subsection{Descriptive statistics}

Table~\ref{tab:descaea} shows descriptive statistics of the surveyed AEAs. There is no significant difference between treatment arms in their basic characteristics (Panel A). On average, they assist six to eight farmer groups. We also measured the AEAs' prosocialness, motivation, and locus of control, and found no significant difference at baseline; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:scales} gives the details of these measurements. Regarding knowledge of rice production, there is no significant difference across treatment arms on the 0--5 scale. Both knowledge indices are built from the AEAs' answers to five questions on recommended practices: seed selection, land levelling, planting spacing, water management, and drying. The 0--5 index gives one point for each correct answer, and the seed-selection question, which allows multiple answers, earns its point only when the AEA names all four valid purposes of seed selection. The 0--8 index instead counts how many of the four purposes the AEA names (0--4) and adds the other four items as they are. When the score is stretched from the 0--5 to the 0--8 scale in this way, a significant difference appears between T1 and the control group (4.25 against 3.10). Nonetheless, there is no difference in the other variables, including training experience, self-reported average monthly visits to farmer groups, and job satisfaction. Overall, there are no systematic differences in AEA characteristics between the treatment and control groups.

Table~\ref{tab:descfarmer} shows descriptive statistics of the surveyed farmers. A typical sample household is headed by a man in his late forties and has six to seven members, and it cultivates a rice plot of about two hectares. At baseline, average yields in the 2024 season were only 0.9 to 1.3 tons per hectare, and 13.5\% of control households lost their crop entirely. About 80\% of farmers applied herbicides or insecticides, while chemical fertilizer was used by only half to two-thirds of them. Machinery, mostly tractors and power tillers for land preparation, was used by 73\% of control farmers but by only 38\% in T1 and 50\% in T2. On average, farmers earned a positive income from rice, between 1,400 and 4,600 GHS per hectare, but once family labor is valued at the local hired wage, profit becomes negative in all three arms. This is because rice farming in our sample relies heavily on family labor, a large part of which is spent on bird scaring.

Unlike the AEAs, farmers in the three arms differ in some areas at baseline. Compared with the control group, T1 farmers used two to three times as much hired and family labor per hectare and earned a higher income, and both treatment groups experienced crop failure less often. Dibbling was much more common in T1 (54\% against 5\% in the control group), and treated farmers more often planted improved varieties. Treated farmers were also less likely to have had any contact with their AEA in the 2024 season (67\% in T1 and 77\% in T2 against 92\%), although those who had contact were somewhat more satisfied with the service. These differences reflect the fact that we randomized only eight districts, so that differences in the production environment across districts are not fully balanced out. To account for this, we control for the baseline value of each outcome and for region fixed effects in the impact analysis (Section~\ref{sec:impacts}).

\section{Measuring AEA performance}\label{sec:performance}

AEAs' efforts and performance are hard to observe for both supervisors and researchers. While some studies rely on AEAs' self-reported efforts, such as the number of visits to their assigned communities \citep[e.g.,][]{alam_incentives_2024}, we show later that AEAs tend to overstate their effort levels in the absence of an effective monitoring system. In this study, we conducted a phone survey of all sample farmers, the direct recipients of extension services, every two weeks between mid-April and the end of September 2025 (eleven rounds in total). During the intervention period, feedback reports were produced from the survey results and shared with each AEA in the treatment groups (both T1 and T2). In this section, we first present descriptive results of the surveys and then compare the control and treatment groups with regression analysis.

\subsection{Descriptive results}

Figure~\ref{fig:bwbasic} shows, for each round of the survey, the average number of AEA visits to farmers, the number of contacts with farmers (both online and face-to-face), and the level of satisfaction with the service (1 = lowest, 5 = highest). In April, the average number of visits in two weeks was around 0.5 per farmer in T1 and in the control group, while it was higher in T2. The number of visits rises from June and peaks in early July. This period, from June to early July, is the planting season in our study areas, when farmers presumably need advice the most. AEAs may also have visited communities more often to disseminate the production technologies they learnt as part of the project. Overall, T2 farmers received more visits than T1 and control farmers. The number of contacts follows a similar trend. One key difference is that control farmers had fewer contacts with their AEA than T1 and T2 farmers did in most rounds. The average level of satisfaction improved over the survey period in all groups. On average, it was highest among T1 farmers, followed by T2 farmers and then control farmers.

Figure~\ref{fig:selfrep} compares, for each AEA, the number of visits reported by farmers with the number of visits the AEA reported in the baseline and endline surveys. Both are converted to a monthly basis: visits per farmer group for the AEA's self-report and visits per farmer for the farmers' reports. On average, AEAs reported that they visited each group nine times a month at baseline and thirteen times at endline. These numbers exceed the visiting frequency expected of AEAs.\footnote{During our field visits, district officers told us that AEAs are supposed to visit each community at least eight times a month.} However, farmers reported only around 1.5 visits per month on average, and almost all AEAs lie below the 45-degree line, which implies that AEAs overstate their efforts. \subsection{Regression analysis}

We investigate how the treatments relate to the efforts and performance of AEAs as reported by farmers. We estimate the following model by OLS:
\begin{equation}
Y_{ijdt} = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 T1_d + \alpha_2 T2_d + \alpha_3 GPS_j + \delta_t + \mu_r + \varepsilon_{ijdt},
\label{eq:bw}
\end{equation}
where $Y_{ijdt}$ is the outcome (AEAs' efforts and performance) reported by farmer $i$, served by AEA $j$ in district $d$, in survey round $t$. $T1_d$ and $T2_d$ are dummies for the treatment arms, and $GPS_j$ is a dummy for AEAs with a GPS stamps treatment. $\delta_t$ are round fixed effects, which absorb the seasonal pattern of extension activity seen in Figure~\ref{fig:bwbasic}. $\mu_r$ are region fixed effects. Standard errors are clustered at the district level. Since we have only eight clusters, we also report $p$-values from the wild cluster bootstrap with Webb weights \citep{webb_reworking_2023} and from randomization inference, which re-assigns the district arms 1,000 times. As the bi-weekly survey started together with the interventions, we have no baseline data for these outcomes and cannot control for differences between districts that existed before the interventions. We therefore interpret the results as correlations rather than causal effects.

Table~\ref{tab:bweffort} presents the results. Across all rounds, T2 farmers reported about 0.15 more visits per two weeks than control farmers, which is about 20\% higher than the control group average. The difference between T1 and the control group is negligible. The relationships become clearer when we restrict the sample to the planting season (June to the first half of July): both T1 and T2 farmers reported significantly more visits than control farmers. These differences are significant under randomization inference ($p=0.017$ for T1 and $p<0.001$ for T2), but not under the wild cluster bootstrap ($p=0.188$ and $0.107$). In contrast, no significant differences are observed for the number of contacts. Finally, the GPS stamp intervention does not show any significant differences.

Across the sample and rounds, fertilizer and planting were mentioned the most (92 and 82 times, respectively), followed by harvest (55 times), weeding (51 times), land preparation (48 times), seed (41 times), and information access (19 times).} These relationships are robust under randomization inference but not under the wild cluster bootstrap. Overall, the regression results suggest that the interventions for AEAs are associated with both a larger quantity and a better quality of the extension services delivered.

\section{AEA and farm level impacts}\label{sec:impacts}

\subsection{Estimation model}

To estimate the impacts of the interventions on AEAs and farmers, we use the baseline and endline surveys. We employ an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) rather than a difference-in-differences (DID) model, because ANCOVA gives more precise estimates than DID when the outcome is weakly correlated over time \citep{mckenzie_beyond_2012}, as is often the case for farm income and profit.

For the AEA-level analysis, we estimate
\begin{equation}
A_{jd1} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 T1_d + \beta_2 T2_d + \beta_3 GPS_j + \zeta A_{jd0} + \varepsilon_{jd},
\label{eq:ancova_aea}
\end{equation}
where $A_{jd1}$ is the outcome of AEA $j$ in district $d$ in the endline survey and $A_{jd0}$ is the same outcome in the baseline survey.
For the farm-level analysis, we estimate
\begin{equation}
F_{ijd1} = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 T1_d + \gamma_2 T2_d + \gamma_3 GPS_j + \eta F_{ijd0} + \varepsilon_{ijd},
\label{eq:ancova_farm}
\end{equation}
where $F_{ijd1}$ is the outcome of farmer $i$, served by AEA $j$ in district $d$, in the endline survey (the 2025 season), and $Y_{ijd0}$ is the same outcome in the baseline survey (the 2024 season). In both equations, $T1_d$, $T2_d$ and $GPS_j$ are defined as in equation~(\ref{eq:bw}). As in Section~\ref{sec:performance}, standard errors are clustered at the district level, and we also report $p$-values from the wild cluster bootstrap and randomization inference. Income and profit can be negative, so for these outcomes the columns labelled ``log'' use the inverse hyperbolic sine transformation.
