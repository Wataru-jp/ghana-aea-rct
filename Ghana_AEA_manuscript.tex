\section{Introduction}

Addressing global food insecurity and poverty, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa, requires sustained improvements in agricultural productivity. Such improvements depend not only on the development of agricultural technologies but also on their effective dissemination and adoption by farmers. Public agricultural extension services play a central role in this process, as knowledge spillovers among farmers can lead to the under provision of privater extension services \citep{Takahashi2020}. Yet public extension services in developing countries often suffer from weak service delivery, including agent absenteeism and infrequent visits to farmers \citep{Anderson2007, Alam2024}. Even when extension agents possess relevant technical knowledge and operational resources, the effectiveness of their services depends on the effort they exert in reaching farmers and supporting technology adoption. This raises a central question: How can extension agents be motivated to deliver more effective services?

This question reflects a broader incentive problem in public service delivery in developing countries. Absenteeism among teachers and health workers is widespread \citep{Chaudhury2006}, and even when present, providers often fail to deliver services commensurate with their knowledge \citep{Das2007, Finan2017}. Because providers’ performance is difficult to measure and monitoring systems are weak in many developing countries, individual effort may be only loosely linked to rewards: working harder often brings neither higher pay nor better promotion prospects, which weakens providers’ incentives to perform well. Previous research has shown that monetary rewards, monitoring, and public recognition can improve service providers' behavior \citep{Muralidharan2011, Duflo2012, Banerjee2008, Ashraf2014, Leaver2021, Mansuri2023}.  
 %and that compensation and prosocial motivation shape who enters public service \citep{Bo2013, Banuri2016}. 
 In addition, providers may exert less effort when they believe that their work is unlikely to improve the lives of the those they serve \citep{Kaur2023}, whereas interventions that emphasize the social value of their work can increase effort \citep{Khan2025}. Similar challenges arise in agricultural extension. \citet{Ragasa2013} report that a lack of encouragement from supervisors undermines extension agents' motivation, whereas expressions of gratitude from farmers strengthen it. These findings suggest that non-monetary interventions that communicate farmers' evaluations or emphasize the social value of extension work may encourage extension agents to exert greater effort. However, whether such interventions change agents' actual behavior and improve outcomes for farmers remains insufficiently understood.

This study uses a randomized controlled trial (RCT) to examine the causal effects of non-monetary interventions on the behavior of public agricultural extension agents (AEAs) and on farmer outcomes in Ghana. Specifically, we introduce two interventions: feedback from farmers and training that communicates the social value of extension work. The first intervention provides AEAs and their supervisors with regular feedback based on aggregate assessments by sampled farmers of the services provided by each AEA, without identifying individual respondents. This feedback may operate through several channels. By making farmers' assessments visible to AEAs and their supervisors, it may provide agents with a sense of recognition for their work. By reporting each agent's previous performance alongside district office averages and the highest average number of visits per farmer among peers, the feedback also facilitates performance comparisons over time and across agents \citep{Blanes2011, Ashraf2014}. Moreover, sharing the feedback with supervisors may strengthen supervisor monitoring. The second intervention consists of training that highlights the role extension services in improving farmers' livelihoods and agricultural productivity. The training is designed not only to reinforce agents' recognition of the social significance of their work but also to strengthen their internal locus of control---that is, their belief that their own efforts and actions can make a meaningful difference to farmers' outcomes. Comparing the feedback-only group with the control group identifies the effect of feedback, while comparing the group receiving both feedback and training with the feedback-only group identifies the additional effect of training.

In addition, we cross-randomize a GPS-stamp intervention with the two interventions described above. AEAs assigned to the GPS-stamp intervention are asked to record their locations when visiting farmers' fields using a smartphone-based form that they must activate themselves. Although recording is requested, compliance is not directly linked to rewards or penalties. This distinguishes our intervention from monitoring interventions examined in previous studies, which have often linked monitoring to financial incentives, such as recording teachers' attendance with photographs and linking it to their salaries \citep{Duflo2012}, or combining digital monitoring with performance-based pay \citep{Genicot2022}. The GPS-stamp intervention is designed to examine the effects of GPS-based monitoring on AEAs' behavior, independently of the feedback and training interventions.

The experiment is conducted in eight districts across four rain-fed rice-growing regions of Ghana---Oti, Bono East, Western North, and Eastern---covered by the Ghana Rice Production Improvement Project (GRIP), implemented by the Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA) since 2022. We randomly assign the eight districts, and thus the 42 participating AEAs working in them, to three groups: three districts receive feedback from farmers only (T1); three receive both feedback and training on the social value of extension work (T2); and two serve as a control group (C), receiving neither intervention. Randomization is conducted at the district level because agents within the same district report to the same district director and work from the same office, creating a risk of spillovers if they are assigned to different groups. Separately, within each district, we randomly assign approximately half of the agents to the GPS-stamp intervention, resulting in 20 of the 42 agents being assigned to this intervention. This ensures that agents with and without GPS stamps are represented in all three experimental groups. All agents in the three groups, including the control group, also received a small fuel allowance of 150 Ghana cedis per month (less than US\$15) through mobile money from April to August 2025. This allowance replaced similar support from a previous project that was phased out in early 2025, and was provided to keep agents’ operating conditions comparable between the baseline and endline periods. Because it was common to all groups, it does not affect the comparison across arms.

Feedback is provided to AEAs and their supervisors every two weeks from mid-April through September in 2025, the main rice-growing season. It is based on a biweekly phone survey of farmers conducted across all three groups, comprising 11 rounds over this period. Farmers associated with AEAs in the control group are surveyed in the same way, but the control-group AEAs and their supervisors do not receive feedback. The feedback reports each AEA's average number of visits per farmer, alongside their previous performance and that of other AEAs in the same district office. It also provides farmers' satisfaction ratings from 1 to 5 and their reasons for satisfaction or dissatisfaction with the extension services received. Baseline and endline surveys of AEAs, conducted in February 2025 and January 2026, respectively, measure their extension activities and psychological characteristics, including prosocial and intrinsic motivation as well as internal locus of control. We also randomly select approximately 400 households from the farmers served by the sample AEAs and collect data on satisfaction with extension services, knowledge and adoption of rice-farming technologies, rice productivity, and household income through baseline and endline surveys. The biweekly phone surveys provide repeated, farmer-reported measures of extension visits and other contacts, satisfaction with extension services, and the content of advice received, independently of the AEAs' own reports. Combining these data allows us to examine how non-monetary interventions and monitoring affect AEAs' psychological characteristics and behavior, as well as outcomes for farmers.

Three features of the design warrant comment. First, the number of participating agents is modest, which limits statistical power for agent-level outcomes and for comparisons across the six cells of the cross-randomized design. The biweekly farmer survey partly mitigates this limitation by providing repeated observations of each agent's activities over 11 rounds, potentially improving the precision of our estimates. Given the small number of agents and district-level clusters, we report district-clustered standard errors together with wild cluster bootstrap and randomization-inference $p$-values. We treat the main effects of feedback, training, and GPS stamps as our primary analyses. \footnote{textcolor{red}{The interaction between feedback and GPS stamps and the analysis of mechanisms will be conducted later}}. Second, because farmers in the control group are also surveyed regularly, control agents may become aware that their visits are being recorded. Such awareness could affect their behavior, potentially reducing the estimated differences between treatment and control groups. Third, because all agents received a small fuel allowance during the intervention period, our estimates should be interpreted as the effects of non-monetary incentives in a setting where agents receive limited support for transportation. 

Our results yield four main findings. First, both interventions improved the quantity and quality of extension services as reported by farmers. During the planting season, farmers served by agents in T1 and T2 reported 0.22 and 0.27 more visits per two weeks, respectively, against a control mean of 0.77. Over the full season, however, this increase was confined to T2. Farmers in both treatment groups were also more satisfied with the services and more likely to report receiving helpful advice, even conditional on the number of visits. Second, the interventions did not affect agents’ intrinsic, extrinsic, or prosocial motivation but strengthened their internal locus of control—specifically regarding agricultural production among trained agents—and improved their knowledge of recommended practices. Agents receiving both interventions also reported fewer visits, consistent with feedback narrowing the gap between self-reported and farmer-reported effort. Third, we find no effects on rice yields or income in the single season covered by the endline survey, but the interventions reduced complete crop failure and altered input and labor use. Farmers used less seed and fewer agrochemicals and substituted hired for family labor, mainly at harvest, increasing profits when family labor is valued at the hired wage. In contrast, GPS stamping without associated rewards or penalties had no detectable effects on agents or farmers. Finally, agents’ self-reports are a poor measure of effort: they substantially overstated visits relative to farmers’ reports, the two measures were uncorrelated, and only farmer-reported visits declined with distance to the communities served. Given randomization across only eight districts, several estimates are significant under randomization inference but not under the wild cluster bootstrap; we therefore interpret the findings as suggestive.

This study makes three contributions. First, it contributes to the literature on agricultural extension in developing countries by focusing on the incentives faced by public extension agents. Existing experimental and non-experimental studies have primarily examined whether technical training improves farmers' technology adoption and productivity, and how the selection of training participants affects knowledge spillovers among farmers \citep[e.g.,][]{Kondylis2017, Ben2019, Beaman2021}. A related literature examines incentives for agricultural extension providers \citep{Takahashi2020}, including lead farmers \citep{Balew2023, Ben2019, Shikuku2019, Okello2023} and public extension agents. Among studies of the latter, \citet{Alam2024} compare monetary rewards with recognition from district directors in Bangladesh, and \citet{Genicot2022} examine pay-for-performance schemes linked to a digital monitoring system in Punjab, Pakistan. Our feedback intervention differs in that it conveys the assessments of service recipients rather than supervisors, and relies on neither monetary rewards nor formal performance-based incentives. By tracing the effects of this feedback, together with training and low-cost visit recording, from agents' activities to farmer outcomes, we provide evidence on whether such approaches can improve both the delivery and the effectiveness of public extension services. 

Second, the study contributes to the measurement of extension-agent performance. Agents' effort is difficult to observe directly, and previous studies have relied mainly on self-reports and administrative records. Self-reports may be subject to overreporting, raising concerns about reliability. Administrative records can capture the number of visits but are less informative about the content of advice or its usefulness to farmers. In this study, we conduct biweekly surveys of farmers in all experimental groups during the intervention period. These surveys provide farmer-level measures of the frequency of agents' visits and other contacts, farmers' satisfaction with extension services, and the content of advice received, independently of agents' own reports. We find that agents' self-reported visits far exceed, and are uncorrelated with, farmers' reports, raising concerns about the reliability of self-reports as a measure of agents' actual effort. %\textcolor{red}{For agents assigned to GPS stamps, we also obtain location-based visit records, which allows us to compare three measures based on different sources for this subsample: agents' self-reports, farmers' reports, and GPS records. Although GPS records are available only for agents whose behavior may itself respond to the intervention, this comparison enables us to assess the extent to which each measure captures agents' effort and deviations across measurements. Combining farmers' reports with location-based records submitted by extension agents may offer a low-cost approach to monitoring and evaluating extension services. Thus, this contribution has implications not only for research measurement, but also for the management and performance evaluation of public extension services.}

Finally, the study contributes to the broader literature on the motivation of public service providers. Studies of teachers and health workers have shown that incentives and beliefs about the impact of one's effort can influence service providers' behavior \citep{Duflo2012, Ashraf2014, Banuri2016, Mansuri2023, Kaur2023, Khan2025}. Non-monetary interventions are often presumed to work by changing providers' motivation or beliefs, yet relatively few studies measure these psychological characteristics directly alongside providers' behavior and beneficiaries' outcomes. We measure agents' intrinsic, extrinsic, and prosocial motivation and their internal locus of control before and after the interventions, and combine these measures with farmers' reports of agents' behavior and with farmer outcomes. This allows us to examine not only whether non-monetary interventions change providers' behavior, but also whether they do so by strengthening providers' motivation or by changing their beliefs about whether their own efforts make a difference to those they serve. Our findings point to the latter channel.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides background on agricultural extension in Ghana. Section 3 describes the experimental design and data. Section 4 examines the measurement of extension-agent performance using farmer-reported data. Section 5 presents the estimated effects of the interventions on extension agents and farmers. Section 6 concludes.

\section{Agricultural extension in Ghana}\label{sec:context}

Agriculture plays a central role in Ghana's economy, employment, rural livelihoods, and food security. In 2025, agriculture accounted for 21.3\% of GDP and employed 34.6\% of the country's workforce \citep{worldbank_wdi_2026}. Agricultural production is dominated by smallholder farmers, many of whom rely on rain-fed farming and face constraints related to irrigation, access to improved technologies, and agricultural services \citep{gipc_agriculture_2025}. Improving agricultural productivity is therefore an important priority for economic development and poverty reduction.

Rice is one of Ghana's major staple foods, alongside maize, and its consumption has increased rapidly with population growth, urbanization, and changing dietary preferences. Despite the expansion of domestic production, Ghana remains heavily dependent on rice imports, with domestic production meeting only 55.1\% of national demand in 2024 \citep{ndpc_progress_2025}. Rice is cultivated under both irrigated and rain-fed conditions, predominantly by smallholder farmers. Many improved rice cultivation practices developed in Asia can be adapted to African conditions, suggesting substantial scope for productivity gains through their wider dissemination and adoption \citep{Otsuka2024}. Strengthening agricultural extension services is therefore an important component of efforts to increase domestic rice production and improve farmers' productivity and profitability. 

In Ghana, public extension services are coordinated by the Ministry of Food and Agriculture (MoFA) and delivered primarily through decentralized agricultural offices. According to \citep{MoFA2025Extension}, Ghana's national farmer-to-extension-agent ratio exceeds 1,500 farmers  per AEA, compared with a benchmark of 500 farmers per agent. During field visits to the study areas in early 2025, interviews with local agricultural officials and extension agents indicated that some agents were responsible for dozens of communities, serving farmers cultivating rice and other crops, but lacked sufficient funding for motorcycle fuel and maintenance. These constraints made it particularly difficult to visit more distant communities regularly. In addition to these resource constraints, weak incentives may limit agents' efforts to deliver advice and support farmers' adoption of improved practices \citep{Ragasa2013, Takahashi2020}. To strengthen rice extension, Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA) and MoFA are implementing the Ghana Rice Production Improvement Project (GRIP, 2022--2027), which provides technical training to extension agents and supports the dissemination of improved rice cultivation practices, including seed selection, land leveling, planting spacing, water management, and drying \citep{jica_grip_2022}. Building on these efforts to strengthen technical capacity, our study examines whether non-monetary incentives and monitoring can further improve extension agents' service delivery and outcomes for farmers.

\section{Experimental design and data}\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Experimental design}

In collaboration with JICA and the Institute of Statistical, Social and Economic Research (ISSER), University of Ghana, we conducted an RCT involving AEAs and associated farmers in rice-producing areas of Ghana. During the study period, the project covered ten sites under JICA's GRIP, comprising eight rain-fed rice-producing districts and two irrigation schemes. In this paper, we focus only on the rain-fed districts.
 
Each AEA serves multiple communities, including five to eight rice-producing areas. While their assigned communities generally do not overlap across different AEAs, they frequently exchange information with colleagues within the same district, which could generate spillovers if treatment were assigned at the AEA level. We therefore randomized treatment assignment at the district level, assigning the eight rain-fed districts to the following three groups: T1 (feedback only; three districts), T2 (feedback plus training; three districts), and a control group (two districts). In addition, within each district, we randomly selected approximately half of the AEAs (20 of 42 in total) to receive a cross-cutting GPS-stamp intervention. Figure~\ref{fig:assignment} summarizes the experimental design, and Figure~\ref{fig:map} shows the study sites and their assigned treatment status.

Specifically, we implemented the following interventions from mid-April to the end of September 2025, covering the main rice production season.
 
\textbf{Feedback}: The first intervention consisted of biweekly feedback reports based on farmers' assessments of their AEA's services. From the second half of April to the end of September 2025, we conducted a brief biweekly phone survey of sampled farmers in all three experimental groups. The questionnaire covered (1) extension activities---whether and how often AEAs had visited the farmers or their communities during the preceding two weeks; (2) satisfaction---farmers' satisfaction with the extension services provided during that period from 1 (not satisfactory at all) to 5 (very satisfactory); and (3) the reasons for their satisfaction or dissatisfaction. Because farmers in the control group were surveyed in the same way, comparisons between the treatment and control groups capture the effect of receiving feedback rather than the effect of being surveyed.
 
Based on the responses of the sampled farmers associated with each AEA (approximately ten farmers per AEA), we prepared a one-page PDF feedback report for each AEA in T1 and T2 and shared it with the AEA and their supervisor within two weeks. The report presented the AEA's average number of visits per farmer during the preceding two weeks, alongside the corresponding average from the previous survey round, the district average, and the highest average among AEAs in the district. It also reported aggregated satisfaction ratings and included farmers' stated reasons for satisfaction or dissatisfaction verbatim, without identifying individual respondents. Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:feedback} shows examples of the feedback reports. By communicating farmers' assessments to AEAs and their supervisors and enabling comparisons over time and across AEAs within the same district, the intervention was intended to encourage effort through performance feedback, social recognition, and accountability.
 
\textbf{Training}: We invited the 15 AEAs in T2 to an overnight sensitization training held on April 29, 2025 at a hotel in Koforidua, the capital of Ghana's Eastern Region. The training was delivered by two assistant directors of the Directorate of Agricultural Extension Services (DAES) of the Ministry of Food and Agriculture (MoFA). It did not provide technical instruction on rice production; instead, it emphasized the social value of agricultural extension and how AEAs' own efforts could improve farmers' livelihoods. All 15 invited AEAs attended. The training was intended to strengthen AEAs' recognition of the social significance of their work and their belief in their ability to make a difference through their own efforts. Similar interventions have been studied among community health workers in Pakistan \citep{Khan2025} and teachers in India \citep{Kaur2023}. Because the training was offered only in combination with feedback, the comparison between T2 and T1 captures the additional effect of training among AEAs receiving feedback.
 
\textbf{GPS stamp}: Within each of the eight districts, we randomly selected approximately half of the AEAs (20 of 42 in total) and asked them to record their GPS coordinates using a KoboToolbox form whenever they visited farmers. The procedure required only a smartphone; GPS coordinates were captured automatically when AEAs opened the form to record a visit. These records were collected for research purposes and were neither shared with supervisors nor linked to rewards or penalties. Nevertheless, the recording requirement could have increased AEAs' awareness that their activities were being monitored, thereby encouraging greater effort. Between mid-April and the end of September 2025, 19 of the 20 AEAs submitted at least one record, yielding a total of 357 records. Because these records exist only for AEAs assigned to the GPS stamp, they cannot be used as outcome measures for comparisons with other AEAs.
 
\textbf{Fuel support}: In addition to interventions above, we provided all 42 AEAs with a monthly fuel allowance of 150 Ghana cedis (less than US\$15 at the 2025 exchange rate) via mobile money between April and August 2025 to help cover the fuel costs of their motorbikes. AEAs were required to submit receipts for fuel purchases. A previous bilateral aid project had provided similar support to all district agricultural offices but was phased out in early 2025; we introduced this allowance to help maintain comparable operating conditions after that program ended. Because the allowance was provided to all AEAs regardless of treatment assignment, it does not confound comparisons across experimental groups. However, if the treatments and fuel support are complementary---for example, if AEAs can increase their visits only when fuel costs are covered---our estimates should be interpreted as treatment effects conditional on this support.

\subsection{Data}

In January and February 2025, prior to the baseline survey, we constructed the sampling frame in three steps.\footnote{
The sampling frame was constructed by a team led by
the ISSER, University of Ghana, with support from Japanese experts working on JICA's
GRIP.} First, we asked the directors of the eight District Departments of Agriculture to provide complete list of AEAs whose assigned communities included rice-producing communities, along with the communities they served. This identified 42 AEAs. Second, we contacted each AEA and asked them to list all the rice-producing communities they served. This yielded 144 communities (Achiase: 11; Bibiani-Anhwiaso-Bekwai: 38; Biakoye: 19; Krachi West: 20; Lower Manya Krobo: 4; Pru West: 11; Sefwi Wiawso: 27; and Sene West: 14). Third, we asked each AEA to provide a list of eight randomly selected rice farmers from each rice-producing community they served. 

We selected farmers separately for each AEA. For AEAs serving multiple rice-producing communities, we randomly selected two communities and then randomly selected five farmers from the list of eight provided for each selected community, yielding ten sampled farmers per AEA. The remaining three farmers on each list were retained as replacements, and enumerators were instructed to interview a replacement if an initially selected farmer could not be interviewed. For AEAs serving only one rice-producing community, all eight farmers on the list were selected for the survey. If fewer than eight rice farmers were available in such communities, we interviewed all of them. Similarly, in communities selected from among those served by AEAs covering multiple communities, we interviewed all available rice farmers if fewer than five were available. This procedure yielded a target sample of 42 AEAs and 396 farmers.

The baseline and endline surveys, biweekly phone serveys, and interventions were implemented by a field research team.\footnote{The fieldwork and interventions were implemented by a team led by a professor at the Department of Agricultural Economics and Agribusiness, University of Ghana, with support from Japanese experts working on JICA's GRIP.} We monitored data quality through both on-site and remote checks. 

\textbf{Biweekly survey data}: To generate feedback reports and measure AEAs' extension activities, we conducted biweekly phone surveys of sample farmers in all three experimental groups from mid-April to the end of September 2025. These surveys provide measures of AEAs' extension-service performance from the perspective of the farmers they serve. Across 11 survey rounds, we collected 4,120 observations, representing more than 99\% of the targeted 4,158 farmer-round observations ($378 \times 11$).

\textbf{Baseline and endline survey data}: We evaluate the effects of the interventions on AEAs and farmers using the baseline survey, conducted from 18 February and 27 March 2025, and the endline survey, conducted between 12 January and 6 February 2026. Table~\ref{tab:obs} compares the target and actual sample sizes. We interviewed all 42 AEAs at baseline and 41 at endline, as one AEA had retired before the endline survey. Among farmers, 378 were interviewed at baseline, of whom 373 were interviewed at endline, corresponding to an attrition rate of 1.3\%.

\subsection{Descriptive statistics}

Table~\ref{tab:descaea} presents descriptive statistics for the surveyed AEAs by experimental group. At baseline, basic characteristics are broadly similar across the experimental groups (Panel A). On average, AEAs serve six to eight farmer groups. We also measured prosocial orientation, work motivation, and locus of control, with no statistically significant differences across groups at baseline. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:scales} describes these measures in detail.

We constructed an index of AEAs' knowledge of recommended rice cultivation practices using five questions covering seed selection, land leveling, planting spacing, water management, and drying. The index ranges from 0 to 5, assigning one point for each correct answer. For the seed-selection question, which permits multiple responses, a point is awarded only if the AEA identifies all four valid purposes.

No statistically significant baseline differences are detected in previous training participation, self-reported average visits to farmer groups, or job satisfaction. Overall, the baseline characteristics of AEAs are broadly comparable across experimental groups, with the exception of the 0--8 knowledge index.

Table~\ref{tab:descfarmer} presents descriptive statistics for the surveyed farmers. A typical sample household is headed by a man in his late forties, has six to seven members, and cultivates a rice plot of approximately two hectares. At baseline, average yields in the 2024 season ranged from only 0.9 to 1.3 tons per hectare across the three experimental groups, and 13.5\% of control households experienced complete crop failure. About 80\% of farmers used herbicides or insecticides, whereas only half to two-thirds applied chemical fertilizer. Agricultural machinery, primarily tractor and power tillers used for land preparation, was employed by 73\% of control farmers, compared with 38\% in T1 and 50\% in T2. Average net returns from rice cultivation, before accounting for the opportunity cost of family labor, were positive across all three groups, ranging from GHS 1,400 to 4,600 per hectare.
However, when family labor is valued at the prevailing local wage for hired agricultural labor, estimated profits become negative in all three groups. This calculation reflects the substantial reliance on family labor, particularly for bird scaring, but may overstate its opportunity cost if alternative employment opportunities are limited or seasonal.

Unlike the AEAs, farmers in the three experimental groups exhibit several differences in baseline characteristics and outcomes. Compared with the control group, T1 farmers used twice as much hired labor and more than twice as much family labor per hectare, and earned higher income from rice cultivation. Farmers in both treatment groups were less likely to experience complete crop failure. Dibbling was substantially more common in T1 (54\% versus 5\% in the control group), and farmers in both treatment groups were more likely to plant improved rice varieties. Farmers in the treatment groups were also significantly less likely to have had contact with their AEA in the 2024 season (67\% in T1 and 77\% in T2, compared with  92\% in the control group), although those who had contact reported somewhat higher satisfaction with extension services. These baseline differences may reflect variation in agricultural production environments across districts, which was not fully balanced by randomization across only eight districts. To improve precision and account for observed baseline differences, our impact analysis controls for the baseline values of each outcome and includes region fixed effects (Section~\ref{sec:impacts}).

\section{Measuring AEA performance}\label{sec:performance}

AEAs' efforts and performance are difficult for both supervisors and researchers to observe. Previous studies have relied on AEAs' self-reported efforts, such as the frequency of visits to their assigned communities \citep[e.g.,][]{Alam2024}, but such measures may be subject to reporting bias. As shown below, we find substantial discrepancies between AEAs' self-reported visits and farmers' reports, raising concerns about the reliability of self-reports. To measure extension-service delivery from the perspective of its recipients, we conducted biweekly phone surveys of sample farmers in all three experimental groups from mid-April to the end of September 2025 (eleven rounds in total). The survey results were used to prepare feedback reports, which were shared with AEAs and their supervisors in T1 and T2 but not in the control group. In this section, we first present descriptive results from the surveys and then examine differences in extension-service delivery across experimental groups using regression analysis.

\subsection{Descriptive results}

Figure~\ref{fig:bwbasic} shows, for each biweekly survey round, the average number of AEA visits per farmer, the number of contacts with AEAs (both remote and face-to-face), and farmers' satisfaction with extension services (on a scale from 1 to 5). In April, farmers in T1 and the control group received approximately 0.5 visits over two weeks, while those in T2 received more visits. Visits frequency increased from June and peaked in early July, coinciding with the planting season in our study areas, when farmers likely had greater demand for extension advice. AEAs may also have visited communities more frequently to disseminate rice production practices promoted by JICA's GRIP. Overall, farmers in T2 received more visits than those in T1 and the control group. The number of contacts followed a similar seasonal pattern, with control farmers reporting fewer contacts than farmers in T1 and T2 in most rounds. Average satisfaction with extension services generally increased over the survey period, although it influenced across rounds. Across the survey period, satisfaction was highest in T1, followed by T2 and the control group.

\subsection{Regression analysis}

We estimate the intention-to-treat effects of the interventions on extension-service delivery as reported by sample farmers. Specifically, we estimate the following model by OLS:
\begin{equation}
Y_{ijdt} = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 T1_d + \alpha_2 T2_d + \alpha_3 GPS_j + \delta_t + \mu_r + \varepsilon_{ijdt},
\label{eq:bw}
\end{equation}
where $Y_{ijdt}$ denotes an outcome reported by farmer $i$, served by AEA $j$ in district $d$, in survey round $t$. $T1_d$ and $T2_d$ indicate assignment to the feedback-only and feedback-plus-training groups, respectively, while $GPS_j$ indicates assignment to the GPS recording intervention. $\delta_t$ and $\mu_r$ denote survey-round and region fixed effects, respectively. The round fixed effects account for common seasonal variation in extension activities, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:bwbasic}. We cluster standard errors at the district level, the unit of randomization for T1 and T2. Given the small number of districts (eight), we also report $p$-values from the wild cluster bootstrap using Webb weights \citep{webb_reworking_2023} and from randomization inference, based on 1,000 reassignments of district-level treatment arms. 

Although we did not conduct a separate baseline survey for the biweekly outcomes, the first round of the phone survey was completed before the feedback reports were delivered and before the training took place. Our main specification includes all eleven survey rounds. As a robustness check, we exclude the first round from the estimation sample and control for each farmer's corresponding first-round outcome. The results remain similar. 

%We therefore interpret the results as correlations rather than causal effects.

Table~\ref{tab:bweffort} presents the estimated treatment effects on farmer-reported visits and contacts. Across all survey rounds, farmers in T2 reported 0.15 more visits per two weeks than those in the control group, an increase of approximately 29\% relative to the control mean of 0.51. The estimated effect of T1 is close to zero. During the planting season (June to the first half of July), both T1 and T2 farmers reported more visits than control farmers, with estimated increase of 0.22 and 0.27 visits per two weeks, respectively. These estimates are statistically significant under randomization inference ($p=0.017$ for T1 and $p<0.001$ for T2), but not under the wild cluster bootstrap ($p=0.188$ and $0.107$, respectively). Similarly, the positive estimates for T2 over the full survey period is significant under randomization inference ($p=0.022$) but not under the wild cluster bootstrap ($p=0.187$). We find no statistically significant effects on the number of contacts or from the GPS-recording intervention. Given the limited number of district-level clusters and the sensitivity of statistical significance to the inference method, we interpret the evidence of increased visits with caution.

\section{AEA- and farm-level impacts}\label{sec:impacts}

\subsection{Estimation model}

To estimate the impacts of the interventions on AEAs and farmers, we use the baseline and endline survey data. We employ an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) specification rather than a difference-in-differences (DID) specification because ANCOVA is generally more efficient when outcomes are weakly correlated over time \citep{mckenzie_beyond_2012}, as is often the case for farm income and profit. Controlling for baseline outcomes also helps account for the baseline differences across experimental groups documented in Section ~\ref{sec:design}. 

For the AEA-level analysis, we estimate
\begin{equation}
A_{jd1} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 T1_d + \beta_2 T2_d + \beta_3 GPS_j + \zeta A_{jd0} + \mu_r + \varepsilon_{jd},
\label{eq:ancova_aea}
\end{equation}
where $A_{jd1}$ and $A_{jd0}$ denote the outcome of AEA $j$ in district $d$ in the measured at endline and baseline, respectively.

For the farm-level analysis, we estimate
\begin{equation}
F_{ijd1} = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 T1_d + \gamma_2 T2_d + \gamma_3 GPS_j + \eta F_{ijd0} + \mu_r + \varepsilon_{ijd},
\label{eq:ancova_farm}
\end{equation}
where $F_{ijd1}$ and $F_{ijd0}$ denote the outcome of farmer $i$, served by AEA $j$ in district $d$, measured at endline (the 2025 agricultural season) and baseline (the 2024 agricultural season), respectively. 

In both equations, $T1_d$, $T2_d$ and $GPS_j$ are defined as in equation~(\ref{eq:bw}). As in Section~\ref{sec:performance}, standard errors are clustered at the district level, and we also report $p$-values from the wild cluster bootstrap and randomization inference. Because income and profit can take negative values, we use the inverse hyperbolic sine (IHS) transformation for these outcomes in specifications with transformed dependent variables.

\subsection{AEA-level impacts}
Panel A of Table \ref{tab:aeaimpact} presents the estimated impacts on AEAs' motivation and locus of control. We find no statistically significant effects on intrinsic, extrinsic, or prosocial motivation. In contrast, T1 has a positive and statistically significant effect on general locus of control (1.83 points), whereas the corresponding effect of T2 is small and insignificant. The difference between T1 and T2 is statistically significant ($p=0.002$). For agriculture-specific locus of control, both T1 and T2 have positive and statistically significant estimated effects (2.36 and 3.00 points, respectively). The difference between the two treatment groups is not statistically significant ($p=0.255$). Feedback from farmers may have strengthened AEAs' belief that their own efforts can make a difference to farmers' outcomes. The sensitization training may have further reinforced this belief in the context of agricultural production, although the absence of a significant difference between T1 and T2 precludes a firm conclusion about its additional effect. 

Panel B shows the impacts on AEAs' knowledge, training attendance (any training on rice production), self-reported visits, and job satisfaction. T2 has a positive impact on knowledge, measured by their answers to five questions with multiple choices on rice production (e.g., What do you think the best water level during reproductive stage?), while T1 does not have a statistically significant impact. The difference between the two treatment groups is statistically significant ($p=0.002$). T2 also has a significant impact on training attendance, although the overall attendance rate has increased since the baseline because of GRIP's activities. Furthermore, T2 increased job satisfaction by about 17\% relative to the control group. T1 does not have a statistically significant impact, and the difference from T2 is statistically significant ($p=0.076$). T1 has a negative impact on AEAs' self-reported visit frequency, although it is significant only at the 10 percent level. As shown in Section~\ref{sec:performance}, however, self-reported visits are not a reliable measure of extension service delivery. We find no significant impact of the GPS stamp treatment. The mediation analysis (Panel C) suggests that the increase in agriculture-specific locus of control brought about by sensitization training has, to some extent, led to enhanced knowledge, higher attendance to rice production training, and higher job satisfaction.

\subsection{Farm-level impacts}

Finally, Table \ref{tab:farmimpact} shows the farm-level impacts. Neither T1 nor T2 has a statistically significant impact on rice yield (column 1 and 2, Panel A). Nonetheless, both significantly reduced the probability of crop failure, by 5.6 and 3.2 percentage points, respectively (column 3). No impact is found on farm income. Regarding farm profit, which takes family labor costs into account, both treatments show a positive and statistically significant impact. On average, they increased farm profit by 3.5\% and 3.7\%, respectively. These effects are consistent when we exclude family labor costs associated with bird scaring, the activity that accounts for the largest share of family labor. There are no statistically significant differences between these two treatment groups in terms of their impacts on crop failure and profit. No statistically significant effect was found for the GPS stamp treatment.

To investigate the drivers of positive impacts of interventions on farm profit, Panel B shows the impacts on input use. Both T1 and T2 reduced applied seed quantity. In the endline, the control farmers used on average 99 kg of seed per hectare, which is higher than recommended amount of 60 to 80 kg per hectare for direct seeding. In both treatment groups, their applied seed quantity got closer to the recommended amount (column 2). They have also reduced the use of herbicide at statistically significant level. Contrarily, T2 has increased the use of machinery by 13.7 percentage points. In our bi-weekly survey, some farmers reported that AEA provided information on machine access. Additionally, both T1 and T2 have increased the hired labour used by about 30.6 and 20.0 man-days per hectare, respectively. Among T2 group, farmers reduced use of family labour. Overall, given that both T1 and T2 do not have negative impact on yield but positive impact on profit, these two interventions enhanced efficiency of input usage presumably through AEA's advice.
